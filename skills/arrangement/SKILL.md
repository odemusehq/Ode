# Arrangement

Arrangement decides what plays when. The same eight bars of music can be a forgettable loop or a song that pulls the listener from the first bar to the last; the difference is how energy and interest change over time, and how clearly the important part (usually the vocal or the hook) is heard at every moment.

Which sections a song has and how long they are is song form. This skill takes those sections and decides what happens inside and between them; when the form isn't settled yet, agree it with the musician first.

The musician is the author. Propose a plan, make one section or one transition at a time, explain each change briefly, and keep the earlier version recoverable. Leave locked parts alone: you can arrange around a locked part (mute other parts against it, add a transition before it), but don't change its notes, clips or devices.

## How to work

1. **Read the song as it is.** Sections and locators, tempo and time signature, every track and what it does, the lead sheet (what the song is about, where the hook is), and which parts are locked.
2. **Lay out what plays where.** Make a simple overview: parts down the side, sections across the top, a mark where each part plays. Gaps and crowded spots become obvious at a glance.
3. **Rate each section's energy from 1 to 10** and compare neighbours. A song feels flat when neighbouring sections have nearly the same energy, and exhausting when everything is at 9.
4. **Find the problem the musician is describing** ("the second verse drags", "the drop doesn't hit") in that overview and energy curve, and name the cause in plain words.
5. **Propose small, specific changes**: what to add, remove or move, in which bars, and why. Offer three options when the direction is open, with one clearly labelled as outside the artist's usual style (judged only from their own songs and stated preferences).
6. **Protect the earlier version.** Before larger rearrangements, keep the original: duplicate the clips to Session view, keep them on a muted copy of the track, or ask the musician to save a version of the Set.
7. **Make the change, then listen through the transition into and out of it**, not just the section itself. A change that works in isolation can break the flow.

## Energy

Energy is how intense a moment feels. Many things raise or lower it, and using several together makes a change obvious:

| Lever | Lower energy | Higher energy |
|---|---|---|
| Density | Few parts | Many parts, layers, doubles |
| Low end | Kick and bass out or filtered | Full kick and bass |
| Rhythm | Half-time, long notes, sparse hats | Driving eighths or sixteenths, busier percussion |
| Register | Lower, narrower range | Higher melody, octave doublings, wider range |
| Stereo width | Narrow, centred | Wide pads, panned doubles and percussion |
| Brightness | Filtered, darker | Open filters, more top end |
| Harmonic rhythm | Chords change slowly | Chords change faster |
| Vocal | Low register, softer delivery, single voice | Higher register, harmonies, doubles, ad-libs |
| Level | Quieter | Louder (the smallest lever; contrast in arrangement matters more) |

**Contrast is what makes a section big.** A chorus feels huge because the pre-chorus held something back. If the musician wants a bigger chorus, look first at what can come out of the section before it.

**Example energy curves (starting points, not rules):**
- Verse–chorus song: intro 3, verse 4, pre-chorus 5, chorus 7, verse 5, pre-chorus 6, chorus 8, bridge 4 or 6, final chorus 9, outro 3.
- Club track: intro 4, build 5, breakdown 3, build 7, drop 9, break 4, build 7, drop 10, outro 4.

More example forms with section lengths and durations at common tempos are in `references/forms-and-timing.md`.

## Adding and removing parts

- **Every section should change something.** A new part, a part removed, a new rhythm, a new register. Listeners notice change; they stop noticing repetition.
- **Arrange by subtraction when you have a full loop.** Build the densest section (chorus or drop) first, then derive the other sections by removing parts. This keeps everything consistent and makes contrast easy.
- **Arrange by addition when you start sparse.** Start with the core (vocal and one instrument, or drums and bass) and add one element every 4 or 8 bars.
- **One focal point at a time.** At any moment, one thing should lead: the vocal, a lead synth, a guitar riff. When two parts compete for attention, let them trade (call and response) or drop one.
- **Fill the gaps, not the phrases.** Counter-melodies, fills and ear candy belong in the spaces between vocal phrases, not on top of the words.
- **Removing is as strong as adding.** Dropping the kick for a bar, cutting the bass before a chorus, or stopping everything for a beat often lifts more than a new layer.

## Roles and frequency allocation

Give every part one role and a register of its own:

| Role | Typical parts | Usual register |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation | Kick, bass, 808 | Sub and low end (below about 120 Hz for the fundamentals) |
| Rhythm | Snare, clap, hats, percussion, rhythm guitar, stabs | Mids and highs |
| Harmony bed | Pads, chords, keys, strings | Low mids to upper mids, voiced away from the vocal |
| Lead | Vocal, topline, lead synth, hook | Mids and upper mids, the most exposed range |
| Counter-melody | Second line, answering phrases | A register above or below the lead |
| Texture and ear candy | Noise, atmospheres, risers, one-off effects | Wherever there is space |

When two parts collide (masking), fix it in the arrangement before reaching for EQ: move one part up or down an octave, change a chord voicing or inversion, change one part's rhythm so they don't hit together, or drop one of them in that section. Check masking between the parts after the change. EQ carving is the mix's job for whatever overlap remains.

Keep chords out of the vocal's main octave where possible; thin the voicing or move it up or down. Keep only one part in the sub at a time.

## Builds

A build raises tension so the next section lands. Common tools, often combined:
- Rhythmic acceleration: snare or clap repeats moving from quarter notes to eighths to sixteenths over the last 4–8 bars.
- Filter movement: a high-pass rising on the music, or a low-pass opening up.
- Risers: noise sweeps, rising tones, pitch going up.
- Removing the low end in the last bar or two, so its return is the impact.
- Harmonic tension: a held dominant chord, a pedal note under changing chords, or holding the chord before the resolution.
- Vocal tools: a pickup phrase, a repeated word or chop, rising harmonies.
- Growing reverb or delay sends so the sound gets bigger and less defined just before the drop.

Typical lengths are 4, 8 or 16 bars. A build that is too long for the payoff, or that uses every tool at once, deflates the drop.

## Breakdowns

A breakdown resets the energy so the song can build again.
- Remove drums and low end; leave a thread (a pad, the vocal, a reduced hook) so the song doesn't stop dead.
- Reveal something: the hook on a new instrument, a new chord colour, an exposed vocal line.
- Keep it short enough to hold attention; 8 or 16 bars is common in club tracks, a few bars in a song.

## Drops and choruses

The arrival must deliver what the build promised.
- Bring kick and bass back exactly on the downbeat.
- A short silence or a cut just before the downbeat (one beat to half a bar) makes the arrival hit harder.
- Mark the downbeat: a crash, an impact or a snare hit.
- Put the hook in its strongest register and make it the clear focal point.
- If the drop feels weak, check the section before it first: is it too full, too loud or too long?

## Transitions

Transitions tell the listener a change is coming and hide the seams between sections: risers and downlifters, reverse cymbals and reverse reverbs that end on the downbeat, drum fills, filter sweeps, short silences, delay throws on the last word of a phrase, impacts and crashes, tape stops, and vocal pickups into the next section. Place them by bar and beat: reverse sounds end exactly on the downbeat; impacts start on it. How to build each one in Live and where to place it is in `references/transitions.md`.

Many transitions use automation (a filter sweep, a send rising). If you can't write the automation yourself, give the musician the exact moves: which parameter, which bars, from which value to which value. Moves made with clips (splitting, muting, moving, adding a one-off clip) work without automation.

## Variation

Repetition makes a hook memorable; unchanged repetition makes a song boring. Keep the hook, vary what surrounds it.
- Change something every 4 or 8 bars: a fill at the end of each phrase, a part dropped for a bar, a variation of the drum pattern.
- Make the second verse different from the first: a new counter-melody, a different drum pattern, half-time feel, fewer or different parts.
- Make the last chorus the biggest: added harmonies or doubles, an octave lift in the melody or the instruments, extra percussion, wider stereo; sometimes a breakdown chorus first (stripped back), then the full one.
- Vary repeated loops with small changes: a different last bar, a note held over the bar line, a different chord inversion, a filter or effect change.
- Endings matter: decide whether the song ends cold (a final hit), fades, or winds down by removing parts.

## From a loop to a full song

1. **Name the parts in the loop** and their roles (foundation, rhythm, harmony, lead, texture).
2. **Agree the form and length** with the musician: sections in order, bar counts, and the resulting duration at the tempo (formula below). Consider where it will be heard: for streaming, listeners usually reach the vocal or hook early; for DJ use, intros and outros of 16–32 bars led by drums make mixing easier. These are contexts to discuss, not rules.
3. **Lay out sections with locators**, named the way musicians read them (Intro, Verse 1, Pre-Chorus, Chorus, Drop, Breakdown, Bridge, Outro).
4. **Build the densest section first** from the full loop.
5. **Derive the other sections by removing parts**, aiming for the agreed energy curve.
6. **Write what is missing**: a part for the breakdown, a fill, a counter-melody for the second verse, transition sounds. Offer these as suggestions; new musical material goes through the musician.
7. **Add transitions** at every section change.
8. **Add variation** inside long sections and across repeats.
9. **Listen through from the start** at a normal level, and adjust what drags or rushes.

If the loop lives in Session view, scenes can stand for sections; record or copy them into the Arrangement once the order is clear. To lengthen or shorten a section without breaking alignment, insert or delete time across all tracks rather than moving clips one by one.

## Timing

In 4/4: **seconds = bars × 240 ÷ BPM** (in general, bars × beats per bar × 60 ÷ BPM).
- 16 bars at 120 BPM = 32 s; 16 bars at 124 BPM ≈ 31 s; 32 bars at 128 BPM = 60 s.
- A 3:30 song at 100 BPM is about 87.5 bars; a 6-minute track at 124 BPM is about 186 bars.

Round section lengths to musical phrase lengths (usually multiples of 4 or 8 bars) and let the total fall where it falls; a phrase that is cut short to hit a duration feels wrong.

## Checks

- Energy changes between neighbouring sections; the biggest moment comes late and is the biggest.
- Every section has a reason to exist and changes something.
- The focal point is clear at every moment; the vocal has space.
- Only one part in the sub at a time; no parts colliding in the same register (check masking after big changes).
- Transitions land on the downbeat; reverse sounds end on it; nothing clicks or cuts off at section edges.
- The total length suits the song and where it will be heard.
- Locked parts unchanged; the earlier arrangement is recoverable.

## Worked examples

**"The second verse drags and the last chorus doesn't lift."** A 100 BPM pop song; overview shows verse 2 is a copy of verse 1 plus a pad, and the last chorus is a copy of chorus 2. Energy: V1 4, Ch1 7, V2 5, Ch2 7, final Ch 7. Three options:
1. Vary verse 2: switch the drums to a half-time feel for its first 8 bars, add a short counter-melody in the gaps between vocal phrases, and drop the pad until the pre-chorus.
2. Shorten verse 2 from 16 to 8 bars so the song reaches the chorus sooner, and lift the final chorus with doubled backing vocals, an octave-up instrument line and one bar of silence before it.
3. **Outside your usual style:** strip the final chorus's first half to vocal, piano and claps, then bring everything in for the second half.
Make one change, then listen from the end of chorus 1 through verse 2.

**"Turn this 8-bar loop into a 6-minute club track."** At 124 BPM, 6 minutes is about 186 bars; a 192-bar form (6:12) keeps every section a multiple of 8. Plan: intro 32 bars (drums building), first build 16, breakdown 16, build 16, drop 32, break 16, build 16, second drop 32, outro 16 (drums mixing out). Build the drop from the full loop, derive the rest by subtraction, and suggest one new element for the breakdown (a pad or a filtered version of the vocal chop). Place locators first, then one section at a time.
