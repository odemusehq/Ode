# Pop harmony

Only what is specific to pop. Function, cadences, borrowing, voicing and the checks are in the harmony skill.

## Loops are normal

Many pop songs run two to four chords through the whole song. The craft is in what changes around the loop, not in adding chords for their own sake. Common loops (common property):

| Loop (C major) | Numerals | Character |
|---|---|---|
| C – G – Am – F | I–V–vi–IV | open, anthemic |
| Am – F – C – G | vi–IV–I–V | the same chords starting darker |
| C – Am – F – G | I–vi–IV–V | classic |
| F – C – G – Am | IV–I–V–vi | starts away from home, floats |
| Am – F – C – G in A minor | i–♭VI–♭III–♭VII | minor-key pop and dance |
| C – F, or Am – F | two-chord vamps | groove and topline carry the song |

The same four chords feel different depending on where the loop starts and which chord the melody treats as home.

## Making sections contrast over the same loop

Use one or two of these per section change:

1. **Start the loop on another chord** in the chorus: verse `Am – F – C – G`, chorus `F – C – G – Am`.
2. **Change the harmonic rhythm**: two bars per chord in the verse, one in the chorus.
3. **Change the bass**: hold a tonic pedal under the verse (`Am – F/A – C/A – G/A`) and release it in the chorus; or use inversions for a stepwise line (`C – G/B – Am – F`).
4. **Change the voicing and register**: low, close voicings in the verse; wide, high ones in the chorus.
5. **Take the chords out**: bass and vocal only in the verse, chords entering at the pre-chorus.
6. **Add one new chord in one place**: a borrowed iv or ♭VII in the last bar before the chorus.

## Pre-chorus

- Move to subdominant chords (IV, ii) and end on V, IV or a sus chord, not on I, so the chorus downbeat is the release.
- Rising bass lines add push: `| Dm7 | Em7 | Fmaj7 | Gsus4 G |` climbs by step and ends on the dominant.
- A bar of IV/V (F/G) right before the chorus is a soft dominant that pop uses constantly.

## Chorus

- Starting on I sounds like arrival; starting on IV lifts without fully landing; starting on vi gives a bittersweet weight.
- If the chorus repeats, end its first pass on V or IV so it turns around; close on I only at the very end of the last chorus, if at all.

## Pop colours

- **add9 and sus2** (Cadd9, Csus2): open and modern. **sus4 resolving to the 3rd**: a small push inside one chord.
- **maj7** for softness, more common in pop R&B and ballads.
- **Slash chords**: IV/V (F/G) as a soft dominant; I/3 (C/E) and V/7 (G/B) for smooth bass lines.
- **Borrowed ♭VII** (B♭ in C) for a bright lift; **♭VI–♭VII–I** (A♭–B♭–C) as a climactic cadence; **iv** (Fm in C) for a bittersweet turn in the last line of a chorus or in a bridge.
- **III7 to vi** (E7–Am in C): a secondary dominant with a dramatic pull.

## Modal pop

- **Mixolydian** (`C – B♭ – F – C`): earthy, rock-pop.
- **Dorian** (`Am – D`, a minor tonic with a major IV): a lifted, soulful minor.
- **Aeolian** loops (`Am – F – C – G` heard in A minor): darker dance-pop and ballads.
- **Lydian** (`C – D/C`): dreamy, floating.

## Bridge

- Go where the song has not been: start on a chord not used yet (often IV, vi or ♭VI), slow down or speed up the harmonic rhythm, or move to the relative minor or major.
- Return through V, or a held IV/V, into the last chorus so the chorus downbeat feels like coming home.

## Final-chorus key change

- Up a half or whole step at the chorus downbeat, prepared by the new key's V or ii–V, or unprepared for a punch.
- Check the melody against the singer's range first: the chorus peak moves up too.
- Other ways to lift without a key change: higher voicings, the full band entering, chords dropping out for the first half then returning, a countermelody or extra vocal harmony.

## Minimal harmony

Some pop runs on two chords or one. Then rhythm, bass movement, sound and the topline carry the interest; resist adding chords only for variety.

## Mood

Avoid the "minor means sad" shortcut. Many heartbroken pop songs sit on bright major loops and many uplifting ones are in minor. Choose chords by colour and by what the lyric is doing.

## Worked example

The musician's chorus loop `| C | G | Am | F |` also runs under the verse, and everything sounds the same. Three directions, each checked against the existing melody before presenting:

1. **Closest**: keep the loop; put the verse over a tonic pedal (`C – G/C – Am/C – F/C`), add a pre-chorus `| Dm7 | Em7 | Fmaj7 | Gsus4 G |`, and let the chorus release into root position.
2. **Colour**: keep the verse; start the chorus on IV — `| F | C/E | Dm7 | Gsus4 G |` — so it lifts and keeps moving instead of landing, and borrow Fm for the final chorus line.
3. **Outside your usual style** (labelled): a Dorian-coloured verse in A minor, `| Am7 | D9 | Am7 | D9 |`, then the chorus back on `C – G – Am – F`. The D major chord's F♯ makes the verse sound lifted; check that the verse melody has no F natural.

## Check (pop-specific)

- Do the sections contrast in something besides the lyric (start chord, bass, harmonic rhythm, voicing, density)?
- Does the pre-chorus avoid landing on I just before the chorus?
- If there is a key change, does the melody stay inside the singer's range?
