# Voicing

How to spread a chord's notes for a given instrument and register. Note names use scientific pitch (middle C = C4, shown as C3 in Live).

## Register

| Region | Roughly | What belongs there |
|---|---|---|
| Low | below C3 | the bass alone: roots, fifths, octaves. Thirds and seconds here sound muddy. |
| Middle | C3 to C5 | the body of the chord: thirds and sevenths, which define its quality |
| High | above C5 | colour tones (9ths, 11ths, 13ths) and the top line |

- Space chords like the harmonic series: wide intervals at the bottom, closer ones at the top.
- Find where the vocal sits before voicing. Keep the chord's top voice below the vocal or clearly above it, and thin out the chord in the vocal's register.
- The top note decides how a chord's colour is heard; the bass note decides its function. A right-hand inversion changes the colour, a bass inversion changes the harmony.

## By instrument

**Piano and keys**
- Left hand: a root, root and fifth, or root and octave below about C3; a shell (root plus 7th, or root plus 3rd) above it.
- Right hand: three or four notes in close position, moving by the smallest steps.
- When a bass instrument plays the roots, leave the root out of the right hand and use rootless voicings (the jazz sub-skill).

**Pads and strings**
- Open, spread voicings: root low, fifth, then the third an octave and a third up, with colour tones on top.
- Slow voice leading, sustained common tones, few changes per bar; fast changes make pads smear.
- Strings: avoid doubling the third in close root-position chords (it sounds heavy), and give each section a line that moves well on its own.

**Guitar**
- Open-position shapes ring and sound full; barre chords are tighter and more even.
- Triads on the top three strings stay clear in a dense mix and leave room for keys and bass.
- Distortion turns thirds muddy, which is why distorted guitars favour power chords (root and fifth).
- A capo changes the key while keeping the same shapes and their open-string sound.

**Synth stabs and plucks**
- Close triads or seventh chords in the middle register, often the same shape moved in parallel (common in house and dance-pop).
- Keep them short so they leave room for the bass and the kick.

## Doubling

- In triads, double the root first, then the fifth.
- In a dominant chord that resolves, don't double the leading tone: both copies want to rise to the tonic and the line becomes awkward.
- Sus and add chords: keep the 2nd, 4th or 9th near the top, not crowded against the root in the low register.

## Voice-leading examples

Keep the top line moving by step and notice which notes hold.

**I–IV–V–I in C, keyboard**
```
C:  C3 | E4 G4 C5
F:  F2 | F4 A4 C5
G:  G2 | D4 G4 B4
C:  C3 | E4 G4 C5
```

**ii–V–I with sevenths, keyboard** — the guide tones (3rds and 7ths) move by half or whole step: C (7th of Dm7) falls to B (3rd of G7); F (7th of G7) falls to E (3rd of Cmaj7).
```
Dm7:   D3 | F4 A4 C5
G7:    G2 | F4 G4 B4
Cmaj7: C3 | E4 G4 B4
```

**Minor loop, pad, open voicings**
```
Am:    A2 | E3 C4 A4
F:     F2 | F3 C4 A4
C:     C3 | E3 C4 G4
G:     G2 | D3 B3 G4
```
Voices move by a step or hold. The top line A4–A4–G4–G4 stays simple, leaving room for a vocal above it.

**Descending bass with inversions, piano**
```
C:     C3  | E4 G4 C5
G/B:   B2  | D4 G4 B4
Am:    A2  | C4 E4 A4
Am/G:  G2  | C4 E4 A4
F:     F2  | C4 F4 A4
```
The right hand moves down by small steps while the bass walks down by step: smooth and song-like.

## Common voicing mistakes

- The same block shape moved in parallel through every chord on a piano ballad — it sounds stiff unless that is the style.
- Thirds below C3, or a dense cluster just under the vocal.
- A chord tone a half step from the melody note, in the melody's octave.
- A top voice that leaps around while the vocal holds a long note.
- Large jumps in the inner voices when a common tone could have held.
