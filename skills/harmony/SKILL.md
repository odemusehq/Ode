# Harmony

Chords give a song its colour, its sense of tension and rest, and its forward motion, and they decide how every melody note feels. Good harmony is not about rare chords: each chord should do a job — support the melody note above it, move the phrase towards its cadence, and fit what the lyric is doing at that moment. The musician is the author: suggest, check, explain briefly, and let them choose.

## Conventions

- Chord symbols: C, Cm, C7, Cmaj7, Cm7, Cm7♭5 (half-diminished), Cdim7, Csus2, Csus4, Cadd9, C6, C/E (C major with E in the bass). Roman numerals show function: I, ii, V7, ♭VII, V/V.
- Note names use scientific pitch: middle C is C4 (MIDI 60); Live's piano roll labels it C3. When you name a note to the musician, add its Live label the first time (for example "E5, shown as E4 in Live") so it matches their piano roll.
- Lengths are in beats of 4/4. A chart line such as `| C | G/B | Am7 | Gsus4 G |` shows one bar per cell; two chords in a cell split the bar equally unless stated.
- Spell chords for the key: in E♭ major the IV chord is A♭ (A♭–C–E♭), never G♯.

## Gather before writing

- Key and mode, tempo, meter, and genre (from the song's settings).
- The melody, if one exists — the biggest constraint. Get its notes and where its strong beats fall.
- The section and its role, and the chords of the other sections.
- What is locked: chords, melody or bass that must stay as they are.
- The artist's chord vocabulary: what they have used, kept or rejected before.
- The bassline and the instruments that will play the chords (piano, guitar, pad), because they shape the voicing.

## How to work

1. Find the melody's strong-beat notes and its phrase endings; decide where cadences go and whether each ends open or closed.
2. Choose chords by function for each phrase, then add colour.
3. Set the harmonic rhythm.
4. Voice the chords: register, voice leading, and the bass note (root or inversion).
5. Check against the melody and the rest of the arrangement (checklist below).
6. Offer two or three options, ordered from closest to the musician's version to boldest, each with a one-line reason. When you offer three, label one clearly as outside the artist's usual style.

## Function: tonic, subdominant, dominant

| Function | Feels | Major (C) | Minor (A) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tonic | home, stable | I C, vi Am, iii Em | i Am, ♭III C |
| Subdominant (predominant) | moving away | IV F, ii Dm | iv Dm, ♭VI F, ii° Bdim |
| Dominant | tension that wants home | V G, V7 G7, vii° Bdim | V E or E7 (from harmonic minor), v Em, ♭VII G |

- The basic motion is tonic → subdominant → dominant → tonic.
- Root movement down a fifth (or up a fourth) is the strongest (ii–V–I). Stepwise roots are smooth (IV–V). Roots a third apart share two notes and sound gentle (I–vi).
- In minor keys, V with its raised leading tone (E7 in A minor, with G♯) gives a strong cadence; v or ♭VII gives a softer, modal one.
- Seventh chords, modes and longer tables are in `references/chord-vocabulary.md`.

## Cadences

| Cadence | Chords (C major) | Effect | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Authentic | V–I, V7–I (G7–C) | complete, final | end of a chorus or the song |
| Plagal | IV–I (F–C); minor plagal iv–I (Fm–C) | gentle, warm; minor plagal is bittersweet | after an authentic cadence, endings |
| Half | ends on V | open, a question | end of a verse or pre-chorus |
| Deceptive | V–vi (G–Am), V–♭VI (G–A♭) | surprise; delays the ending | extend a phrase, hold back closure before the last chorus |
| ♭VII–I | B♭–C | strong but not classical | rock and pop endings |

Match cadences to the melody's phrase endings. An open melodic ending over a closed cadence, or the reverse, sounds confused unless that is the point.

## Harmonic rhythm

Harmonic rhythm is how often the chords change. Faster changes bring motion and urgency; slower changes bring space, groove and weight.

- One chord per bar is the common default; two per bar builds energy, often in a pre-chorus; one chord over two bars suits sparse verses and fast tempos.
- Change chords on strong beats. A change pushed early, onto the "and" of 4, is a deliberate anticipation (common in pop, funk and R&B); the bass and drums should push with it.
- Contrast sections: if the verse holds each chord for two bars, the pre-chorus can change every bar or half bar.
- Keep it steady within a phrase, except when speeding up into a cadence, which sounds natural.

## Borrowed chords

Borrow chords from the parallel key (modal mixture) to change colour without leaving the key.

- In C major, from C minor: iv Fm, ♭VI A♭, ♭VII B♭, ♭III E♭, iiø7 Dm7♭5, v Gm.
- In A minor, from A major: IV D, and a major I (A) as the final chord.
- The sound of iv in major is its ♭6 falling to 5 (A♭ to G in C): `| C | F | Fm | C |`.
- ♭VI and ♭VII bring lift and breadth before the tonic: `| C | A♭ | B♭ | C |`.
- A borrowed chord changes a scale note (A♭ for A, E♭ for E). If the melody holds the original note at the same moment, they clash; check every melody note under the borrowed chord, weak beats included.

## Secondary dominants

A secondary dominant is the dominant of a chord other than I. It strengthens the arrival on that chord.

| In C major | Chord | Leading tone it adds | Resolves to |
|---|---|---|---|
| V/ii | A7 | C♯ | Dm |
| V/iii | B7 | D♯ | Em |
| V/IV | C7 | B♭ (the 7th) | F |
| V/V | D7 | F♯ | G |
| V/vi | E7 | G♯ | Am |

- The added note pulls by a half step towards the target; that pull is the point. Resolve to the target, or deceptively (E7 to F).
- If the melody sits on the note the secondary dominant alters (C natural over A7), they clash, unless the style uses it as a blue note.
- Lighter options: the triad without the seventh, or a secondary ii–V (Em7–A7 into Dm7).

## Modulation

Change key for lift, for a new colour in one section, or for a final-chorus boost.

- **Pivot chord**: a chord that belongs to both keys. C major to G major through Am (vi in C, ii in G): `| C | Am | D7 | G |`.
- **Dominant approach**: play the new key's V, or ii–V, just before arriving. C major up a whole step to D major: `| C | Em7 A7 | D |`.
- **Direct**: start the new key at a section boundary, usually up a half or whole step for the last chorus. A beat or a bar of the new key's V makes it smoother.
- **Relative major or minor**: C major and A minor share every note; moving the tonal centre changes the mood without new chords.
- **Chromatic mediant**: to a key a third away (C to E♭ or A♭) for drama; works best at section boundaries, helped by a common tone.
- Check the singer's range: a key change up a whole step moves the chorus peak up too. Check the melody stays inside the range before suggesting it.

More modulation routes are in `references/chord-vocabulary.md`.

## Reharmonization: keep the melody, change the chords

Start from the melody's strong-beat notes. For each one, list the chords that hold it as a chord tone or a good colour tone, then choose by function and by the bass line they make.

One melody note, many chords — a melody E4 held for a bar in C major:

| Chord | E is the | Feel |
|---|---|---|
| C | 3rd | plain, home |
| Am | 5th | softer, minor colour |
| Em | root | neutral, a little darker |
| Fmaj7 | major 7th | floating, open |
| Dm9 | 9th | soft, moving |
| A7 | 5th | pushes to Dm (secondary dominant) |
| G6 | 6th | bright dominant colour |
| A♭maj7 | clash with its E♭ | avoid while E is held |

Techniques, from subtle to bold:

1. Inversions and bass movement: `C – G/B – Am` keeps the chords and adds a line.
2. Diatonic substitutes that share notes: vi or iii for I, ii for IV.
3. Sevenths, ninths, sus chords.
4. Secondary dominants or ii–Vs into important chords.
5. Borrowed chords.
6. Chromatic passing chords (`C – C♯dim7 – Dm7`) and pedal points (chords changing over a held bass note).
7. A different harmonic rhythm.
8. Jazz devices such as tritone substitution (the jazz sub-skill).

Worked example. A melody in C major holds E through bar 1, D through bar 2, C through bar 3 and D through bar 4; the original chords are `| C | G | Am | G |`.

- **Subtle**: `| C | G/B | Am7 | Gsus4 G |` — the bass falls C–B–A, and bar 4 adds a suspension. E is the 3rd of C, D the 5th of G/B, C the 3rd of Am7, D the 5th of Gsus4 and G.
- **Colour**: `| Cmaj7 | Bm7♭5 E7 | Am7 | F6 G |` — a secondary ii–V into Am in bar 2 (D is the 3rd of Bm7♭5 and the 7th of E7); in bar 4 D is the 6th of F6, then the 5th of G.
- **Outside the usual style**: `| Am7 | Dm9 | A♭maj7 | B♭add9 |` — ♭VI and ♭VII borrowed from C minor, ending on ♭VII, which pulls back to C. E is the 5th of Am7, D the root of Dm9, C the 3rd of A♭maj7, D the 3rd of B♭add9. Check the weak beats too: any E natural in bar 3 would clash with the E♭ of A♭maj7.

## Voicing and voice leading

- Keep common tones in the same voice and move the other voices by the smallest step.
- Prefer contrary motion between the bass and the top voice.
- Register: thirds and seconds below about C3 turn muddy. Keep the bass alone down there, with fifths or octaves at most; put thirds and sevenths in the middle register and colour tones on top.
- The top voice of the chords is a melody too: make it move smoothly, and keep it out of the vocal's register so the two don't fight.
- Avoid putting a chord tone a half step from the melody note in the same octave as the melody.
- Parallel fifths and octaves between the outer voices sound hollow in choral and string writing; in rock power chords and some synth textures they are the sound. Know which texture you are writing.

Example, `| C | Am | F | G |` in close position over a root bass:

```
C:  C3  | E4 G4 C5
Am: A2  | E4 A4 C5
F:  F2  | F4 A4 C5
G:  G2  | D4 G4 B4
```

C5 holds through three chords and every other voice moves by a step or a third; in the last change the upper voices move down while the bass moves up. Voicings by instrument (piano, pads, guitar, synth stabs) and more voice-led examples are in `references/voicing.md`.

## Bass movement and inversions

- **Root position** is the strongest and clearest.
- **First inversion** (third in the bass, C/E) is lighter and good for stepwise bass lines.
- **Second inversion** (fifth in the bass, C/G) is unstable: use it as a passing chord, before a cadence (`C/G – G7 – C`), or over a pedal.
- **Stepwise lines**: falling `C – G/B – Am – Am/G – F`; rising `C – Dm7 – C/E – F – F♯dim7 – G`.
- **Pedal point**: one bass note held while the chords change above it (`C – F/C – G/C – C`); builds tension in intros, pre-choruses and endings.
- **Non-inversion slash chords** colour a chord through the bass: F/G is a soft dominant; D/C puts a tense 7th under D. Tell the musician what the bass note does.
- The bass skill covers rhythm and register; agree with it on the bass note under every chord.

## Harmony and meaning

Fit the chords to what the lyric does at that moment, not to a mood label. "Sad means minor" is a cliché: plenty of heartbroken songs are in major keys and plenty of hopeful ones in minor, and mood comes from tempo, register, rhythm, voicing and words as much as from chord quality. Consider instead:

- an unresolved question → end the phrase on a half cadence, a sus chord, or IV instead of I;
- a turn in the story → a deceptive cadence or a borrowed chord on that word;
- something held back → a pedal point under moving chords, or a faster harmonic rhythm;
- an arrival or a decision → an authentic cadence landing on the key word;
- something bittersweet → iv or ♭VI borrowed inside a major key.

Offer these as options and say why; the musician decides what the words mean.

## Check before presenting

- Every strong-beat melody note is a chord tone or a good colour tone, and no weak-beat note clashes with an altered chord note.
- Cadences fall at phrase ends, open or closed as intended.
- The bass line moves well and inversions are deliberate.
- Voice leading is smooth and the register is clear, with no mud below C3 and no fight with the vocal.
- The harmonic rhythm suits the tempo and the groove.
- Chord symbols are spelled correctly for the key.
- Locked parts are untouched. If a requested chord clashes with a locked melody, name the clashing note and offer alternatives; don't change the melody.
- After a key change the melody is still inside the singer's range.
- Progressions are common property, but never pair one with a melody or rhythm that imitates a known song.

## Presenting

- Show chord charts bar by bar so the harmonic rhythm is visible: `| C | G/B | Am7 | Gsus4 G |`.
- One sentence per option on what changed and why, in musical terms: "Bar 3 borrows Fm so the A♭ falls to G under 'stay'."
- Mention voicing or bass changes only where they matter to the sound.
