# Bassline examples

Notation as in the bass skill: scientific pitch (middle C = C4), lengths in beats of 4/4, `r` = rest, `~` = an overlapping slide, `× n` = the same note repeated n times. Each line is a starting point; check it against the song's real kick pattern and other low parts.

## Contents

1. Pop-rock eighths
2. Ballad with a descending line
3. Funk with octaves and the blues third
4. House off-beat bass
5. Walking bass
6. A longer 808 line

## 1. Pop-rock eighths

G major, 130 BPM, `| G | D | Em | C |`. Steady root eighths drive the song; the last bar turns around with an approach note.

```
| G  | G1(½) × 8 |
| D  | D2(½) × 8 |
| Em | E1(½) × 8 |
| C  | C2(½) × 6, A1(½) F♯1(½) |
```

F♯1 sits a half step below G1 and pulls back to the top. Accent the notes that fall with the kick; play the rest slightly softer.

## 2. Ballad with a descending line

C major, 70 BPM, `| C | G/B | Am | Am/G | F | C/E | Dm7 | G |`, one note per bar.

```
C2(4) | B1(4) | A1(4) | G1(4) | F1(4) | E1(4) | D2(4) | G1(2) G1(1) B1(1) |
```

The inversions turn the chords into a stepwise falling line. The D moves up an octave (D2) because D1 is below a four-string bass and too low to read clearly; the last bar walks up to B1, a step below the C that starts the next phrase.

## 3. Funk with octaves and the blues third

E7, 105 BPM, a one-bar figure in sixteenths. The kick plays on 1 and 3, the snare on 2 and 4.

```
| E7 | E1(½) E2(¼) E1(¼) r(½) D2(¼) E2(¼) E1(½) r(¼) G1(¼) G♯1(½) B1(½) |
```

- The low E lands with the kick on beats 1 and 3; the octave jumps add bounce.
- The rest on beat 2 leaves the snare alone.
- G1 to G♯1 slides from the minor third to the major third, the classic blues and funk move, landing on the chord's 3rd on beat 4.
- Add ghost notes (very short, muted, low velocity) in the gaps for more motion; keep them quiet.

## 4. House off-beat bass

A minor, 124 BPM, kick on every beat. The bass plays the off-beats between the kicks, so the two never mask each other.

```
| Am | r(½) A1(½) r(½) A1(½) r(½) A1(½) r(½) A2(½) |
```

Repeat on each chord's root, keep the notes short (well under an eighth) for a tight bounce, and jump to the octave on the last off-beat to turn the bar around.

## 5. Walking bass

The first four bars of a jazz blues in F, quarter notes, swung feel.

```
| F7       | F1(1)  A1(1)  C2(1)  B1(1)  |
| B♭7      | B♭1(1) D2(1)  F2(1)  E2(1)  |
| F7       | F2(1)  E♭2(1) C2(1)  A1(1)  |
| Cm7  F7  | C2(1)  G1(1)  F1(1)  A1(1)  |
```

Chord tones fall on beats 1 and 3. In bars 1, 2 and 4, beat 4 approaches the next root from a half step away (B1 into B♭1, E2 into F2, A1 into the B♭ of bar 5); bar 3 walks down the F7 chord instead, to vary the direction.

## 6. A longer 808 line

G minor, 150 BPM, half-time feel with the clap on beat 3, `| Gm | Gm | E♭ | D |`.

```
| Gm | G1(2) r(1) G1(½)~D2(½) |
| Gm | G1(1½) r(1) G1(½) B♭1(1) |
| E♭ | E♭2(2) r(1) E♭2(½)~D2(½) |
| D  | D2(2) r(1½) D2(½)~(slides down into the G1 that starts the loop) |
```

- Every bar leaves beat 3 free for the clap.
- Three slides in four bars: up a fifth, down a half step into the D chord, and down into the loop. More would blur the line.
- The E♭ sits up at E♭2 (about 78 Hz) rather than E♭1 (about 39 Hz), where small speakers would lose it.
- Note lengths set the tails: the long notes ring, the short ones stop before the next hit.
