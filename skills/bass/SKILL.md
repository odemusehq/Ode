# Bass

The bass does three jobs at once: it defines the harmony (the bass note decides how a chord is heard), it ties the harmony to the drums (it locks with the kick), and it can carry a counter-melody of its own. A good bassline does the first two without fuss and adds the third only where there is room.

## Conventions

- Note names use scientific pitch: middle C is C4 (MIDI 60), shown as C3 in Live. When you name a note to the musician, add its Live label the first time (for example "E1, shown as E0 in Live") so it matches their piano roll. Low notes for reference: E1 ≈ 41 Hz, A1 = 55 Hz, E2 ≈ 82 Hz, A2 = 110 Hz.
- Lengths are in beats of 4/4: 1 = quarter, ½ = eighth, ¼ = sixteenth, 1½ = dotted quarter, 2 = half note; `r` is a rest and `|` a barline.
- In 808 lines, `~` joins two overlapping notes so the sound glides from the first to the second.
- Chords are chord symbols.

## Gather first

- Key, tempo, meter, genre, and the chords with their harmonic rhythm.
- The kick pattern — the actual notes in the drum clip, not an assumption — and the snare.
- The register and rhythm of the other parts: the keys' left hand, guitar, pads, and the vocal.
- The section and its energy; what is locked.
- The artist's habits: bass sounds and styles they have kept or rejected.

## Following the chords

- Play the root on each chord change, usually on the downbeat. That states the harmony clearly.
- Use chord tones (3rd, 5th, 7th) for movement within a chord, and octaves and fifths for energy without new pitches.
- Connect chords with passing tones (scale steps between chord tones) and approach notes (a half step below or above the next root, just before it arrives) on weak beats.
- A pedal point — one bass note held under changing chords — builds tension in intros, pre-choruses and builds.

## Colouring the chords

The bass note can change what a chord is:

- the 3rd in the bass (C/E) softens a chord and makes stepwise lines possible;
- the 5th in the bass (C/G) is unstable; use it in passing or before a cadence;
- a note outside the triad changes the harmony: A under C makes Am7, F under G makes G7 with its 7th in the bass, G under F makes a suspended F/G.

Do this deliberately, agree it with the harmony skill, and tell the musician what the bass note does to the chord.

## Locking with the kick

- Start bass notes with the kick on the strong hits, especially beat 1. When they hit together, they sound like one powerful instrument.
- Then choose: stay with the kick for weight, or play in the gaps between kicks for bounce (house off-beat bass is the clearest case).
- Avoid long bass notes that smear across every kick; end notes before the next kick when the two would mask each other, or shorten the kick. Ducking the bass with the kick (sidechain compression) is a mixing choice, covered by the mixing skill.
- Design the bass and the kick together, and check the bassline against the real kick pattern before presenting it.

## Rhythm and space

- Rests are part of the groove. A bassline with no space has no bounce.
- Short notes punch and leave room; long notes give weight and warmth. Note length shapes the groove as much as note choice.
- Syncopation — anticipating a chord change on the "and" of 4, or accenting off-beats — adds drive. Keep beat 1 strong so the listener keeps the downbeat.
- A repeated one- or two-bar figure (an ostinato or riff) is often stronger than a line that changes every bar.
- Leave the snare beats lighter than the kick beats; the bass and snare rarely need to hit together.

## Register

- Most basslines live between about E1 and C3. Electric bass spans roughly E1 to G3; synth bass and 808s go lower.
- Sub-bass fundamentals sit roughly between 30 and 60 Hz (about B0 to B1). Below about E1 many speakers lose the fundamental, so make sure the sound has harmonics above it, or move the line up.
- Keep chords out of the low register: intervals smaller than a fifth turn to mud below about C3. Play single notes, octaves or fifths down there.
- Octave jumps up are an energy tool; dropping an octave for a chorus adds weight.
- Keep out of the keys' left hand: if the piano plays roots around C2–C3 too, one of them should move or stop.

## Passing tones and approach notes

- Diatonic passing tones fill a third between chord roots by step: under `| C | Am |`, C–B–A.
- Chromatic approach notes lead into the next root from a half step away on the last eighth or quarter of the bar: G♯ into A, D♭ into C.
- Walking bass (jazz, swing, some blues) plays four quarter notes per bar: chord tones on beats 1 and 3, passing or approach notes on 2 and 4, with an approach note on beat 4 into the next chord.
- Keep passing notes on weak beats so the strong beats still state the harmony.

## Octaves and fifths

- Root–octave patterns in eighths drive disco, funk and dance music.
- Root–fifth patterns (the fifth below or above) give the two-feel of country, rock and folk, and steady movement in pop.
- Skip the fifth on chords whose fifth is altered (diminished, half-diminished, augmented, ♭5); use the root, octave or the chord's own fifth.

## 808 basslines and slides

- An 808 is a long, sine-like bass drum that works as both kick and bass in trap, drill and much modern hip-hop and pop. Tune it so its notes match the key: the long tail is a pitch, and a wrong one clashes with every chord.
- **Note length is the tail**: the length of each MIDI note sets how long the 808 rings. Shorten notes in busy passages, end them before the next hit if a separate kick plays, and let the note before a rest ring out.
- **Slides (glide)**: with the 808 in mono and glide (portamento) on, overlapping two notes makes the pitch slide from the first to the second. Slide up an octave, or to the fifth, for movement; slide back down to land on the root. Use slides as accents, a few per phrase, not on every note.
- **Simple lines win**: 808s often follow only the strongest chord roots, or stay on the key's root while the chords move. If the 808 stays on E under a D chord, it makes D/E, a tense, suspended sound; choose that deliberately.
- **Range**: the most useful 808 notes sit roughly between E1 and E2 (41–82 Hz). Much lower and small speakers lose them; much higher and they stop sounding like a low end.
- **Small speakers**: saturation or distortion adds harmonics so the 808 is heard on phones and laptops; that is a sound-design and mixing choice.

Example, E minor, 140 BPM, half-time feel with the clap on beat 3, chords `| Em | C |`:

```
| Em | E1(2) r(½) E1(½)~E2(1) |
| C  | C2(1½) r(1) C2(½)~G1(1) |
```

The long first note rings through beats 1 and 2, the rest leaves the clap on beat 3 alone, and each bar ends with one slide: up an octave in bar 1, down a fourth in bar 2, which leads back to E1.

## Sub-bass and mid-bass roles

- **Sub-bass** (below about 80 Hz) is felt more than heard: a sine-like tone, mono, simple lines with few fast notes.
- **Mid-bass** (roughly 80–300 Hz plus harmonics) is what you hear on small speakers: character, attack and the notes' identity.
- When layering, give each layer a role: the sub carries the fundamental, the mid layer carries the tone and articulation. Two layers fighting in the same octave turn to mud.
- With a bass guitar and a synth bass together, decide which one owns the sub and keep the other above it.

## How to work

1. Read the chords, the kick pattern, the tempo, the genre and what else plays in the low and mid register.
2. Propose two or three lines that differ in approach — for example roots locked to the kick; a melodic line with passing tones; a syncopated or octave line — each with one sentence on its feel. When you offer three, label one clearly as outside the artist's usual style.
3. Check the line (below), and present it with note names and lengths.
4. The musician hears it before it lands in the Set; new lines go in new clips, and locked parts stay untouched.

## Worked example

Chorus in A minor, 100 BPM, chords `| Am7 | Fmaj7 | C | G |`, kick on beat 1, the "and" of 2, and beat 3; snare on 2 and 4.

1. **Roots, locked to the kick** — solid and simple, with a chromatic approach back to the top:
```
| Am7   | A1(1) r(½) A1(½) A1(1) r(1) |
| Fmaj7 | F1(1) r(½) F1(½) F1(1) r(1) |
| C     | C2(1) r(½) C2(½) C2(1) r(1) |
| G     | G1(1) r(½) G1(½) G1(1) r(½) G♯1(½) |
```
2. **Melodic, with passing tones** — the kick hits keep their roots; beats 3 and 4 add chord tones and passing notes:
```
| Am7   | A1(1) r(½) A1(½) E1(1) G1(1) |
| Fmaj7 | F1(1) r(½) F1(½) A1(1) B1(1) |
| C     | C2(1) r(½) C2(½) G1(1) A1(1) |
| G     | G1(1) r(½) G1(½) B1(1) G♯1(1) |
```
   G1 steps down into F1, B1 up into C2, A1 down into G1, and G♯1 up into A1 at the top of the loop.
3. **Outside your usual style** (labelled) — octaves in eighths, low notes with the kick, the snare beats left empty:
```
| Am7 | A1(½) A2(½) r(½) A1(½) A1(½) A2(½) r(½) A2(½) |
```
   The same figure on F, C and G in the following bars.

All three keep their low notes between E1 (about 41 Hz) and C2, below the keys' left hand; the octave line's upper notes reach C3.

## Check before presenting

- The bass note under each chord is its root, or a deliberate inversion you can name.
- Passing and approach notes fall on weak beats and resolve.
- The rhythm agrees with the real kick pattern where it should lock, and leaves space.
- The register suits the sound: nothing important below about E1 without harmonics, no low chords.
- No clash with the keys' left hand or other low parts; locked parts untouched.
- 808s: tuned to the key, note lengths set so tails don't smear, slides used sparingly.
