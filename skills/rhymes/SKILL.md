# Rhymes

Rhyme serves the song: a tool to use where it helps. It makes lines memorable, gives the ear a sense of arrival, ties ideas together and puts weight on the words it touches. Used carelessly, it drags lines into nonsense or makes a song predictable. The goal is the right rhyme in the right place, in the musician's accent, with the meaning kept whole, with the check describing sound rather than deciding artistic quality: "love" and "move" look alike and sound different; "weight" and "late" look different and rhyme.

## The loop

1. **Read.**
   - The section's lines and its matching section (verse 1 for verse 2) in the lyrics. Run the rhyme check on both: the scheme letters and the kind of every pair.
   - The Pronunciation setting (the song's, else the artist's), and anything the musician has said or Ode remembers about the accent they sing in.
   - The notes the rhyme words are sung on: a rhyme word on a long note needs a vowel that can be held.
   - The lines the lead sheet lists as locked, and the hand edits since the last request (a pair the musician just typed is their newest intent).
2. **Decide** which line to change, what kind of rhyme the moment needs (strong where the section lands, softer where it keeps moving), and which way out of a forced rhyme to take. Meaning comes first: a line that says the right thing with a near rhyme beats a perfect rhyme that changes what the song means.
3. **Change in place.** Re-end or rewrite the fewest lines, usually one line of the pair, in the lyrics: the same line under the same label, saved against the revision you read. When one line of the pair is locked, re-end the other. A chorus line changed in one copy reaches every copy with the same label when the lyrics are saved. A saved strictRhymes preference applies when it still reflects the musician's request; a current choice of near rhyme or no rhyme overrides it. Keep one version in the text, the clips and the takes: Again gives another take and the version history keeps this one.
4. **Verify** every line you changed:
   - **Rhyme check** in the musician's accent: report the kind the tool finds and compare it with the intended sound. Matching-section schemes are references, not obligations; an unrhymed section may be exactly right.
   - **Read the classifier's scope.** Its rhyme span runs from the last stressed vowel to the end; a pair linked only by an unstressed ending ("city" / "me") may count as unrhymed while still providing an intended sound echo.
   - **Originality check:** the changed lines carry fresh pairs, or an over-used pair kept on purpose (a chant-like chorus) that the result line names.
   - **Lyrics-to-melody check and prosody check** on each changed line: inspect the intended syllable setting and emphasis, including melisma or deliberate stress departures.
   - **Report the kind exactly as the check labels it**, with the accent: "(perfect, UK)". A near rhyme is reported as near. A word with two pronunciations ("again", "either") may rhyme in only one of them; say which one the rhyme needs.
   Fix unintended mismatches and check again, up to three rounds; report what the check found without forcing a perfect rhyme or overriding the musician's chosen pair.
5. **Let them hear it.** The result line names the section of the first changed line, so the musician can play it from the locator named like its label.
6. **Report in one line**: what changed · what was checked. For example:
   - "Verse 2, line 4 now ends on 'stone', perfect rhyme with 'phone' in US English · 8 syllables on its 8 notes, ABCB like verse 1."
   - "Chorus now lands on one sound ('late' / 'wait' / 'gate') with the title left unrhymed · perfect (UK), counts fit the notes, fresh rhyme pairs."
   - "Kept 'dance' / 'romance' as you sing them (perfect in your accent, weaker in standard UK English) · 7 syllables on 7 notes."

## Asking

Rhyme fixes go ahead without a question. When a pair works only in another accent than the setting: if the musician has told you (or Ode remembers) that they sing in that accent, keep it and say "rhymes in your accent"; otherwise use the setting to describe the finding, changing it only as the requested rhyme fix warrants. Keep an insisted pair and explain its sound in the checked accent. Keep this song's pronunciation in lead_sheet.settings; note a regional accent in its context when the UK/US control cannot express it. Make pronunciation an artist preference only when explicitly enduring. In Manual, Ode shows the change for Apply or Skip, and you work the same way.

## Kinds of rhyme

A rhyme runs from the last stressed vowel of a word to its end. "Alone" rhymes on "-one", "ceiling" on "-eiling".

| Kind | What matches | Example (original) | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect rhyme | the stressed vowel and everything after it; the sound before it differs | stone / alone; ceiling / feeling | strong, closed, resolved; can feel predictable or sing-song if overused |
| Near rhyme (slant rhyme) | close, slightly different sounds, usually the same vowel with similar consonants | home / stone; cup / luck | modern and conversational; opens up far more word choices |
| Assonance | the vowel sound only | night / time | a subtle link; keeps lines connected without a chime |
| Consonance | the final consonants only, with different vowels | dust / mist | the weakest link; adds texture, works well inside lines |
| Multisyllabic rhyme | two or more syllables, often across several words | paper planes / later trains; broken promise / spoken honest | impressive and musical; central to rap, a strong effect in any style |
| Internal rhyme | rhymes inside a line, or between the middle of a line and its end | I kept the *light* on late at *night* | momentum and density |
| Identical rhyme | the same sound including the consonant before the vowel: the same word, or words that sound alike | night / knight; you / you | weak, unless the repetition is the point (emphasis, a double meaning, a repeated title) |

**Eye rhymes** (love / move, cough / though) match in spelling only. Judge them only by what they sound like.

## How strong a rhyme to use

Perfect rhyme usually gives a clearer chime than near rhyme, assonance or consonance; none is a quality ranking. Closure also depends on delivery, melody and syntax. Possible uses:

- Strong rhymes where the song should land: the end of the chorus, the title line, the final line of the song.
- Softer rhymes where the song should keep moving or sound like speech: verses, pre-choruses, conversational songs.
- An unrhymed line where you want a jolt, or where the honest wording simply ends unrhymed.

## Rhyme schemes

Letters mark which line endings rhyme with which.

| Scheme | Feel | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| AABB (couplets) | punchy, simple, settled; can sound sing-song | choruses, upbeat songs |
| ABAB (alternating) | balanced, flowing | verses and choruses in most styles |
| ABCB (only lines 2 and 4 rhyme) | relaxed, conversational, room for exact wording | verses, ballads, folk and singer-songwriter songs |
| AAAA | insistent, building, intense | rap, pre-choruses, climactic choruses |
| AAAB (three rhymes, then an unrhymed line) | sets up a surprise; the last line stands out and leaves things open | a title line at the end of a chorus; the end of a pre-chorus |
| ABBA (enclosed) | circular, a little formal | occasional verses |
| Unrhymed | spoken, raw, intimate | spoken sections, some bridges |

**Settled and unsettled sections.** An even number of lines with the last line rhymed feels settled. An odd number of lines, or an unrhymed last line, feels unsettled and leans into what comes next. Use this on purpose:

- Verses: settled, or gently unsettled if they hand over to a pre-chorus.
- Pre-chorus: unsettled, so it lifts into the chorus.
- Chorus: usually settled, because it is the arrival. A song about doubt or unfinished business can leave it open on purpose.
- Bridge: a different scheme from verse and chorus, so it feels like new ground.

**Compare matching sections when useful.** Repeating a scheme can reinforce a repeated melody; changing it or leaving a verse unrhymed can redirect attention. Follow the musician's choice.

## Where rhymes go

- The end of a line is the strongest position, so put the important word there. Spend rhymes on words that matter, and keep them off throwaway words ("the", "of", "it").
- Internal rhymes speed lines up and add density; use them for energy, leave them out for calm.
- More rhyme feels more musical, tight or playful; less sounds more conversational and serious. Match the musician's taste and the section's job.

## Meaning first: forced rhymes

Signs that the rhyme is driving the line:
- a word from outside the song's world, there only because it rhymes;
- word order only a rhyme would force ("to the shop went I");
- filler added to reach the rhyme word;
- an image that changes the subject;
- a phrase that would sound strange said to the person the song is about.

Ways out, in roughly this order:
1. **Change the rhyme sound** by re-ending the other line of the pair, so the line you need has better options.
2. **Use a near rhyme** that lets the line say exactly what it means.
3. **Restructure** the line so a different word lands at the end.
4. **Drop the rhyme** where the honest wording or the musician's direction calls for it, changing the scheme if needed.

### Worked example

> *I waited at the station in the rain*
> *And thought about the price of sugar cane*

The second line exists only for the rhyme; sugar cane sits outside the song's world. The musician clicks "Fresher rhymes". Ode keeps line 1, the scene the song is built on, and rewrites line 2 in place with a rhyme word that belongs in the scene: *And counted every late and cancelled train*. The rhyme check (UK) calls "rain" / "train" perfect; the syllable check gives 10|11, which fits the phrase's 10 notes with "every" sung as two syllables. ("Cancelled" is UK spelling; US is "canceled". Follow the setting.) Result line: "Line 2 now 'And counted every late and cancelled train', perfect rhyme with 'rain' (UK) · 10 syllables on its 10 notes with 'every' as 'ev-ry'."

On Again, a different way out: re-end line 1 this time (*I waited on the platform, soaked right through* / *And every face that passed me wasn't you*), which turns the verse toward the person the song is about. "Through" / "you" is perfect in UK English.

## Fresh and over-used pairs

Some pairs are so familiar that listeners hear the rhyme coming before it arrives, and they tend to pull clichés in with them. The originality check labels them; among the most common:

fire / desire, heart / apart, heart / start, love / above, pain / rain, night / light, night / tonight, eyes / skies, eyes / lies, true / you, cry / goodbye, baby / maybe, girl / world, soul / control, fly / sky, away / stay, forever / together, mind / find, me / free, door / more, day / way, tears / years, cold / hold, alone / home.

They are allowed; sometimes a simple, expected rhyme is exactly right, especially in a chant-like chorus. When one appears in a line you change, freshen it, or keep it on purpose and say so. Ways to find fresher rhymes:

- Rhyme a different kind of word: a noun with a verb, an everyday object with a feeling.
- Use a near rhyme on a concrete, surprising word.
- Use a multisyllabic rhyme that spans two words.
- Put the rhyme inside the line and leave the end free.
- Re-end the partner line so the obvious rhyme is out of reach.

## Rhyming across accents

The rhyme check uses the accent in the settings: the UK setting reflects standard Southern British English, the US setting general American English. Many singers have other accents (Northern English, Scottish, Welsh, Irish, Southern US and many more). When the musician says a pair rhymes the way they sing it, believe them, and treat their accent as the reference for that song.

Common pairs that change with the accent, as the rhyme check labels them:

| Pair | UK | US |
|---|---|---|
| bath / path | perfect | perfect |
| bath / math | weaker (consonance) | perfect |
| dance / romance | weaker (consonance; perfect in Northern English accents) | perfect |
| father / bother | weaker (consonance) | perfect |
| calm / bomb | weaker (consonance) | perfect |
| law / more | perfect | weaker (assonance) |
| idea / near | perfect | weaker |
| been / seen | perfect | weaker (consonance) |

When a rhyme works in only one accent, say so in the result line, so the musician knows how it will sound to listeners elsewhere. `references/accents.md` explains the sounds behind these pairs, with more examples.

## Finding rhymes

1. Decide what the line has to say before looking for a rhyme.
2. Gather candidates by sound for the end word with the rhyme check in the musician's accent, and for other end words the partner line could have.
3. Filter by meaning first, then freshness, then singability (an open vowel if the rhyme lands on a long note), then the musician's vocabulary.
4. Write the complete line; a rhyme only works in context. Then run the checks.
