# Rhymes

Rhyme serves the song; it is not a rule. It makes lines memorable, gives the ear a sense of arrival, ties ideas together and puts weight on the words it touches. Used carelessly, it drags lines into nonsense or makes a song predictable. The goal is the right rhyme in the right place, in the musician's accent, never at the cost of meaning, and judged by the rhyme check, never by eye: "love" and "move" look alike and don't rhyme; "weight" and "late" look different and do.

## The loop

1. **Read.**
   - The section's lines and its matching section (verse 1 for verse 2) in the lyrics. Run the rhyme check on both: the scheme letters and the kind of every pair.
   - The Pronunciation setting (the song's, else the artist's), and anything the musician has said or Ode remembers about the accent they sing in.
   - The notes the rhyme words are sung on: a rhyme word on a long note needs a vowel that can be held.
   - The lines the lead sheet lists as locked, and the hand edits since the last request (a pair the musician just typed is their newest intent).
2. **Decide** which line to change, what kind of rhyme the moment needs (strong where the section lands, softer where it keeps moving), and which way out of a forced rhyme to take. Meaning comes first: a line that says the right thing with a near rhyme beats a perfect rhyme that says something the song doesn't mean.
3. **Change in place.** Re-end or rewrite the fewest lines, usually one line of the pair, in the lyrics: the same line under the same label, saved against the revision you read. When one line of the pair is locked, re-end the other. Change a chorus line in every copy of the chorus in the text. Never put alternatives in the text, in clips or in takes; Again gives another take and the version history keeps this one.
4. **Verify** every line you changed:
   - **Rhyme check** in the musician's accent: the pair has the kind you meant (perfect or multisyllabic where the section should land; near rhyme or assonance where it should keep moving), and the scheme letters match the matching section.
   - **Stressed syllables rhyme with stressed syllables.** The check's rhyming part runs from the last stressed vowel to the end; a pair that only matches on an unstressed ending ("city" / "me") isn't a rhyme.
   - **Originality check:** no over-used pair on the changed lines, unless you kept one on purpose (a chant-like chorus) and the result line says so.
   - **Lyrics-to-melody check and prosody check** on each changed line: one of its counts still equals the notes of its phrase, with the stresses on the strong notes.
   - **Report the kind exactly as the check labels it**, with the accent: "(perfect, UK)". Never upgrade a near rhyme to perfect. A word with two pronunciations ("again", "either") may rhyme in only one of them; say which one the rhyme needs.
   Fix and check again, up to three rounds; if the rhyme you need still isn't there, the result line says what the check found.
5. **Let them hear it.** After your turn Ode plays the section of the first changed line, from the locator named like its label to the next locator. You don't start playback.
6. **Report in one line**: what changed · what was checked. For example:
   - "Verse 2, line 4 now ends on 'stone', perfect rhyme with 'phone' in US English · 8 syllables on its 8 notes, ABCB like verse 1."
   - "Chorus now lands on one sound ('late' / 'wait' / 'gate') with the title left unrhymed · perfect (UK), counts fit the notes, no over-used pairs."
   - "Kept 'dance' / 'romance' as you sing them (perfect in your accent, weaker in standard UK English) · 7 syllables on 7 notes."

## Asking

Rhyme fixes don't need a question. When a pair works only in another accent than the setting: if the musician has told you (or Ode remembers) that they sing in that accent, keep it and say "rhymes in your accent"; otherwise follow the setting, fix it, and say which accent you checked in; the history covers the rest. When the musician says they sing in UK or US English, store it as their pronunciation setting; another accent (Northern English, Scottish, Southern US) is remembered about them. In Let me hear it, never ask; in Manual, Ode shows the change for Apply or Skip, and you work the same way.

## Kinds of rhyme

A rhyme runs from the last stressed vowel of a word to its end. "Alone" rhymes on "-one", "ceiling" on "-eiling".

| Kind | What matches | Example (original) | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect rhyme | the stressed vowel and everything after it; the sound before it differs | stone / alone; ceiling / feeling | strong, closed, resolved; can feel predictable or sing-song if overused |
| Near rhyme (slant rhyme) | close but not identical sounds, usually the same vowel with similar consonants | home / stone; cup / luck | modern and conversational; opens up far more word choices |
| Assonance | the vowel sound only | night / time | a subtle link; keeps lines connected without a chime |
| Consonance | the final consonants only, with different vowels | dust / mist | the weakest link; adds texture, works well inside lines |
| Multisyllabic rhyme | two or more syllables, often across several words | paper planes / later trains; broken promise / spoken honest | impressive and musical; central to rap, a strong effect in any style |
| Internal rhyme | rhymes inside a line, or between the middle of a line and its end | I kept the *light* on late at *night* | momentum and density |
| Identical rhyme | the same sound including the consonant before the vowel: the same word, or words that sound alike | night / knight; you / you | weak, unless the repetition is the point (emphasis, a double meaning, a repeated title) |

**Eye rhymes** (love / move, cough / though) match in spelling, not in sound. Judge them only by what they sound like.

## How strong a rhyme to use

From strongest to weakest: perfect, near, assonance, consonance, no rhyme. Stronger rhymes give more sense of closure. Match the strength to the moment:

- Strong rhymes where the song should land: the end of the chorus, the title line, the final line of the song.
- Softer rhymes where the song should keep moving or sound like speech: verses, pre-choruses, conversational songs.
- No rhyme where you want a jolt, or where the honest line simply doesn't rhyme.

## Rhyme schemes

Letters mark which line endings rhyme with which.

| Scheme | Feel | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| AABB (couplets) | punchy, simple, settled; can sound sing-song | choruses, upbeat songs |
| ABAB (alternating) | balanced, flowing | verses and choruses in most styles |
| ABCB (only lines 2 and 4 rhyme) | relaxed, conversational, room for exact wording | verses, ballads, folk and singer-songwriter songs |
| AAAA | insistent, building, intense | rap, pre-choruses, climactic choruses |
| AAAB (three rhymes, then a line that doesn't) | sets up a surprise; the last line stands out and leaves things open | a title line at the end of a chorus; the end of a pre-chorus |
| ABBA (enclosed) | circular, a little formal | occasional verses |
| Unrhymed | spoken, raw, intimate | spoken sections, some bridges |

**Settled and unsettled sections.** An even number of lines with the last line rhymed feels settled. An odd number of lines, or a last line that doesn't rhyme, feels unsettled and leans into what comes next. Use this on purpose:

- Verses: settled, or gently unsettled if they hand over to a pre-chorus.
- Pre-chorus: unsettled, so it lifts into the chorus.
- Chorus: usually settled, because it is the arrival. A song about doubt or unfinished business can leave it open on purpose.
- Bridge: a different scheme from verse and chorus, so it feels like new ground.

**Keep matching sections on the same scheme.** Verse 2 follows verse 1's scheme, because the melody repeats and listeners expect a rhyme wherever the melody's phrase ends.

## Where rhymes go

- The end of a line is the strongest position, so put the important word there. Don't spend a rhyme on a throwaway word ("the", "of", "it").
- Internal rhymes speed lines up and add density; use them for energy, leave them out for calm.
- More rhyme feels more musical, tight or playful; less sounds more conversational and serious. Match the musician's taste and the section's job.

## Meaning first: forced rhymes

Signs that the rhyme is driving the line:
- a word that doesn't belong in the song's world, there only because it rhymes;
- word order no one would use ("to the shop went I");
- filler added to reach the rhyme word;
- an image that changes the subject;
- a phrase you'd never say to the person the song is about.

Ways out, in roughly this order:
1. **Change the rhyme sound** by re-ending the other line of the pair, so the line you need has better options.
2. **Use a near rhyme** that lets the line say exactly what it means.
3. **Restructure** the line so a different word lands at the end.
4. **Drop the rhyme** there if the scheme allows it (an ABCB verse doesn't need lines 1 and 3 to rhyme).

### Worked example

> *I waited at the station in the rain*
> *And thought about the price of sugar cane*

The second line exists only for the rhyme; sugar cane has nothing to do with the song. The musician clicks "Fresher rhymes". Ode keeps line 1, the scene the song is built on, and rewrites line 2 in place with a rhyme word that belongs in the scene: *And counted every late and cancelled train*. The rhyme check (UK) calls "rain" / "train" perfect; the syllable check gives 10|11, which fits the phrase's 10 notes with "every" sung as two syllables. ("Cancelled" is UK spelling; US is "canceled". Follow the setting.) Result line: "Line 2 now 'And counted every late and cancelled train', perfect rhyme with 'rain' (UK) · 10 syllables on its 10 notes with 'every' as 'ev-ry'."

On Again, a different way out: re-end line 1 instead (*I waited on the platform, soaked right through* / *And every face that passed me wasn't you*), which turns the verse toward the person the song is about. "Through" / "you" is perfect in UK English.

## Fresh versus over-used pairs

Some pairs are so familiar that listeners hear the rhyme coming before it arrives, and they tend to pull clichés in with them. The originality check labels them; among the most common:

fire / desire, heart / apart, heart / start, love / above, pain / rain, night / light, night / tonight, eyes / skies, eyes / lies, true / you, cry / goodbye, baby / maybe, girl / world, soul / control, fly / sky, away / stay, forever / together, mind / find, me / free, door / more, day / way, tears / years, cold / hold, alone / home.

They aren't banned; sometimes a simple, expected rhyme is exactly right, especially in a chant-like chorus. When one appears in a line you change, freshen it, or keep it on purpose and say so. Ways to find fresher rhymes:

- Rhyme a different kind of word: a noun with a verb, an everyday object with a feeling.
- Use a near rhyme on a concrete, surprising word.
- Use a multisyllabic rhyme that spans two words.
- Put the rhyme inside the line and leave the end free.
- Re-end the partner line so the obvious rhyme isn't available.

## Rhyming across accents

The rhyme check uses the accent in the settings: the UK setting reflects standard Southern British English, the US setting general American English. Many singers have other accents (Northern English, Scottish, Welsh, Irish, Southern US and many more). When the musician says a pair rhymes the way they sing it, believe them, and treat their accent as the reference for that song.

Common pairs that change with the accent, as the rhyme check labels them:

| Pair | UK | US |
|---|---|---|
| bath / path | perfect | perfect |
| bath / math | weaker (consonance) | perfect |
| dance / romance | weaker (consonance; perfect in Northern English accents) | perfect |
| father / bother | weaker (consonance) | perfect |
| calm / bomb | weaker (consonance) | perfect |
| law / more | perfect | weaker (assonance) |
| idea / near | perfect | weaker |
| been / seen | perfect | weaker (consonance) |

When a rhyme works in only one accent, say so in the result line, so the musician knows how it will sound to listeners elsewhere. `references/accents.md` explains the sounds behind these pairs, with more examples.

## Finding rhymes

1. Decide what the line has to say before looking for a rhyme.
2. Gather candidates by sound for the end word with the rhyme check in the musician's accent, and for other end words the partner line could have.
3. Filter by meaning first, then freshness, then singability (an open vowel if the rhyme lands on a long note), then the musician's vocabulary.
4. Write the complete line; a rhyme only works in context. Then run the checks.
