# Rhymes

Rhyme serves the song; it is not a rule. It makes lines memorable, gives the ear a sense of arrival, ties ideas together and puts weight on the words it touches. Used carelessly, it drags lines into nonsense or makes a song predictable. The goal is the right rhyme in the right place, chosen by ear, for the musician's accent, and never at the cost of meaning.

## Ground rules

- **Rhyme is sound, not spelling.** "Love" and "move" look alike and don't rhyme; "weight" and "late" look different and do. Songs are heard, so only the sound counts.
- **Rhyme follows the accent.** Judge rhymes in the musician's pronunciation setting (UK or US); a pair can be perfect in one accent and weak in the other.
- **Check every pair before you label it.** Call two words a rhyme of a given kind only when the check agrees, and use the check's label. Never judge rhymes by eye. If a check can't run, say the rhymes are unchecked.
- **Meaning comes first.** A line that says the right thing with a near rhyme beats a perfect rhyme that says something the song doesn't mean.
- **The musician is the author.** Offer two or three complete lines rather than rewriting the verse, and never change locked lines. When you offer three, label one clearly as outside their usual style.
- **Write new lines every time.** The examples here only illustrate the craft; never reuse them in the musician's song.

## Kinds of rhyme

A rhyme runs from the last stressed vowel of a word to its end. "Alone" rhymes on "-one", "ceiling" on "-eiling".

| Kind | What matches | Example (original) | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect rhyme | the stressed vowel and everything after it; the sound before it differs | stone / alone; door / more | strong, closed, resolved; can feel predictable or sing-song if overused |
| Near rhyme (slant rhyme) | close but not identical sounds, usually the same vowel with similar consonants | home / alone; cup / luck | modern and conversational; opens up far more word choices |
| Assonance | the vowel sound only | night / time | a subtle link; good for keeping lines connected without a chime |
| Consonance | the final consonants only, with different vowels | dust / mist | the weakest link; adds texture, works well inside lines |
| Multisyllabic rhyme | two or more syllables, sometimes across several words | ceiling / feeling; open the door / hoping for more | impressive and musical; central to rap, a strong effect in any style |
| Internal rhyme | rhymes inside a line, or between the middle of a line and its end | I kept the *light* on late at *night* | momentum and density |
| Identical rhyme | the same sound including the consonant before the vowel: the same word, or words that sound alike | night / knight; you / you | weak, unless the repetition is the point (emphasis, or a double meaning) |

**Rhyme stressed syllables with stressed syllables.** "City" and "me" don't rhyme, because the stress in "city" is on "ci-"; forcing them together puts a stress on "-ty". The same goes for "singing" and "king". If a style tolerates it, say so and leave the choice to the musician.

**Eye rhymes** (love / move, cough / though) match in spelling, not in sound. Judge them only by what they sound like: "love" and "move" share nothing but their final "v".

## How strong a rhyme to use

From strongest to weakest: perfect, near, assonance, consonance, no rhyme. Stronger rhymes give more sense of closure. Match the strength to the moment:

- Strong rhymes where the song should land: the end of the chorus, the title line, the final line of the song.
- Softer rhymes where the song should keep moving or sound like speech: verses, pre-choruses, conversational songs.
- No rhyme where you want a jolt, or where the honest line simply doesn't rhyme.

## Rhyme schemes

Letters mark which line endings rhyme with which.

| Scheme | Feel | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| AABB (couplets) | punchy, simple, settled; can sound sing-song | choruses, upbeat songs, children's songs |
| ABAB (alternating) | balanced, flowing | verses and choruses in most styles |
| ABCB (only lines 2 and 4 rhyme) | relaxed, conversational, room for exact wording | verses, ballads, folk and singer-songwriter songs |
| AAAA | insistent, building, intense | rap, pre-choruses, climactic choruses |
| AAAB (three rhymes, then a line that doesn't) | sets up a surprise; the last line stands out and leaves things open | a title line at the end of a chorus; the end of a pre-chorus |
| ABBA (enclosed) | circular, a little formal | occasional verses |
| Unrhymed | spoken, raw, intimate | spoken sections, some bridges |

**Settled and unsettled sections.** An even number of lines with the last line rhymed feels settled (stable). An odd number of lines, or a last line that doesn't rhyme, feels unsettled (unstable) and leans forward into what comes next. Use this on purpose:

- Verses: settled, or gently unsettled if they hand over to a pre-chorus.
- Pre-chorus: unsettled, so it lifts into the chorus.
- Chorus: usually settled, because it's the arrival. A song about doubt or unfinished business can leave the chorus unsettled deliberately.
- Bridge: a different scheme from both verse and chorus, so it feels like new ground.

The prosody skill covers how line counts and phrase lengths add to this.

**Keep matching sections on the same scheme.** Verse 2 usually follows verse 1's scheme, because the melody repeats and listeners expect a rhyme wherever the melody's phrase ends.

## Where rhymes go

- The end of a line is the strongest position, so put the important word there. Rhymed words draw attention; don't spend a rhyme on a throwaway word ("the", "of", "it").
- Internal rhymes speed lines up and add density; use them for energy and leave them out for calm.
- The more rhyme there is, the more musical, tight or playful the lyric feels; less rhyme sounds more conversational and serious. Match the musician's taste and the section's job.

## Meaning first: avoiding forced rhymes

Signs that the rhyme is driving the line:
- a word that doesn't belong in the song's world, there only because it rhymes;
- word order no one would use ("to the shop went I");
- filler added to reach the rhyme word;
- an image that changes the subject;
- a phrase you'd never say to the person the song is about.

Ways out, in roughly this order:
1. **Change the rhyme sound** by re-ending the other line of the pair, so the line you need has better options.
2. **Use a near rhyme** that lets the line say exactly what it means.
3. **Restructure** the line so a different word lands at the end.
4. **Drop the rhyme** in that spot if the scheme allows it (an ABCB verse doesn't need lines 1 and 3 to rhyme).

### Worked example

> *I waited at the station in the rain*
> *And thought about the price of sugar cane*

The second line exists only for the rhyme; sugar cane has nothing to do with the song. Options:

1. *I waited at the station in the rain / and counted every late and cancelled train* — perfect rhyme
   The rhyme word belongs in the scene, and the detail adds frustration. ("Cancelled" is UK spelling; US spelling is "canceled". Follow the musician's setting.)
2. *I waited at the station in the rain / and watched the platform empty out again* — perfect for speakers who say "a-GAIN", near for those who say "a-GEN"
   Loneliness in a picture; check which way the musician says "again".
3. *I waited on the platform, soaked right through / and every face that passed me wasn't you* — perfect rhyme, with the first line re-ended
   Changing the first line's end word opened a better rhyme, and the line now points at the person the song is about.

## Fresh vs over-used pairs

Some pairs are so familiar that listeners hear the rhyme coming before it arrives, and they tend to pull clichés in with them:

fire / desire, heart / apart, heart / start, love / above, pain / rain, night / light, eyes / lies, soul / control, cry / goodbye, forever / together, dream / seem, baby / maybe, touch / much, true / you, free / me, away / stay.

They aren't banned; sometimes a simple, expected rhyme is exactly right, especially in a chant-like chorus. When one appears, flag it, and make the line around it fresh or turn the expectation on its head. Ways to find fresher rhymes:

- Rhyme a different kind of word: a noun with a verb, an everyday object with a feeling.
- Use a near rhyme on a concrete, surprising word.
- Use a multisyllabic rhyme that spans two words.
- Put the rhyme inside the line and leave the end free.
- Re-end the partner line so the obvious rhyme isn't available.

## Rhyming across accents

The check uses the accent in the musician's settings. The UK setting reflects standard Southern British English and the US setting general American English, but many singers have other accents (Northern English, Scottish, Welsh, Irish, Southern US and many more). When the musician says a pair rhymes in the way they sing, believe them, and treat their accent as the reference for that song.

Common pairs that change with the accent:

| Pair | UK (Southern British) | US |
|---|---|---|
| bath / path | perfect | perfect |
| bath / math(s) | weaker | perfect |
| dance / romance | weaker (perfect in Northern English accents) | perfect |
| father / bother | weaker | perfect |
| calm / bomb | weaker | perfect |
| law / more | perfect | weaker |
| idea / near | perfect | weaker |
| been / seen | perfect for many speakers | weaker |

When a rhyme only works in one accent, say so, so the musician knows how it will sound to listeners elsewhere. `references/accents.md` explains the main differences behind these pairs, with more examples.

## Suggesting rhymes

1. Decide what the line has to say before looking for a rhyme.
2. Gather candidates by sound for the end word, and for alternative end words of the partner line.
3. Filter by meaning first, then freshness, then singability (an open vowel if the rhyme lands on a held note), then the musician's vocabulary.
4. Write complete lines; a rhyme only works in context.
5. Check each pair and label it as the check reports it.
6. Present two or three complete options. Give a bare list of rhyme words only when the musician asks for one.

## Before you present

- Every rhyme label comes from the check, in the musician's accent (or the accent they've told you they sing in).
- The scheme letters are correct, and matching sections share a scheme.
- Stressed syllables rhyme with stressed syllables.
- Over-used pairs and accent-dependent rhymes are flagged.
- Every line still says what the song means, in natural word order.
- Locked lines are untouched.
