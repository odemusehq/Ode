# Rhymes in pop

Pop rhymes help the listener predict and sing along. A good pop chorus feels inevitable: the ear knows roughly where it's going, and the arrival still feels satisfying. The rhymes skill covers rhyme types, schemes and checking; this file adds what is specific to pop.

## Choruses

- **Land the chorus on strong rhymes.** The ends of chorus lines, and above all the title line, are where perfect rhymes pay off: they give the arrival its click. Near rhymes are welcome in verses and increasingly common in choruses, but a chorus with only weak rhymes can feel like it never lands.
- **Or let the title stand alone.** Three rhymed lines followed by an unrhymed title line (AAAB) makes the title jump out because it breaks the pattern.
- **Stay on one or two rhyme sounds.** A chorus that keeps returning to the same vowel works like a hook in itself and is easier to remember and sing back.
- **Rhyme the title with something unexpected,** not its most obvious partner. If the obvious rhyme is the only one that fits, keep the title unrhymed instead.
- **Repetition is allowed.** Repeating the title, which makes an identical rhyme with itself, is normal in pop choruses and post-choruses; it's the hook doing its job, not a weak rhyme.

**Example (original), AAAB with the title unrhymed:**

> *Can't go up and can't come down* (7)
> *Watching all the lights in town* (7)
> *Each new circle comes back round* (7)
> *I'm in a holding pattern* (7)

"Down" and "town" are a perfect rhyme, and "round" is a near rhyme with both (the extra "d"), so the first three lines chime on one sound. The title breaks the pattern and sticks. All four lines have seven syllables, so the melody can repeat its rhythm.

## Pre-choruses

- Internal rhymes and short rhymed phrases build momentum and pull toward the chorus.
- An unsettled scheme (three rhymed lines, or an unrhymed last line) increases the lift; save the strongest closing rhyme for the chorus.

## Post-choruses

- Echo the title's vowel so the two hooks feel connected.
- One or two repeated words are enough; rhyme hardly matters here, rhythm and vowel do.

## Rhyme words on held notes

In pop, the rhyme word often lands on the longest note of the line, so its vowel has to sing well:

- Open vowels hold beautifully: "go / slow", "day / stay", "far", "down". Check these against the over-used pairs list in the rhymes skill; the vowel can be open without the pairing being stale.
- Short vowels with hard endings ("bit", "stuck", "pop") cut the note off. Put them on short notes, or choose a different rhyme sound for held ones.
- Check the vowels against the melody with the prosody skill.

## Freshness in a repeated chorus

A chorus is heard four or more times, so a stale pair is exposed every time. If an over-used pair appears in a chorus, make the rest of the line surprising, or re-end the partner line so the pair is no longer needed.

## Pop rhyme checks

On top of the rhymes skill's checks:
- The chorus lands on a strong rhyme, or deliberately leaves the title line unrhymed.
- The chorus stays on one or two rhyme sounds.
- Rhyme words on held notes have vowels that can be sustained.
- No over-used pair sits in the chorus without a fresh line around it.
