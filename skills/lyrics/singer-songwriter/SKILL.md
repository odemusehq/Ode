# Singer-songwriter lyrics

With a guitar or piano and one voice, every word is exposed. Listeners lean in to the story and the honesty of the voice, so a weak line shows at once. The lyrics skill covers the loop and the general craft; this file offers story-led acoustic approaches, not obligations to sound conversational, tell a linear story or conceal the moral.

## Write for exposure

- For a conversational voice, try each line spoken aloud. Read the lyric as if telling it to someone across a table; anything that sounds like filler or decoration sounds worse when sung.
- Understatement often hits harder than declaration. Trust the listener to feel what the details imply.
- Leave the moral unspoken at the end. If the story has done its work, the listener already knows.

## Story and detail

- Build scenes from specific, ordinary things: a time of day, the weather, an object on a table, what someone said, a name.
- Let characters act. "She folds his shirts and leaves them on the stairs" says more about a relationship than any adjective.
- Use the details only this narrator would know; they make a song feel like lived experience.
- Give the story a shape: each verse a scene, time moving forward, something different by the end.
- When the song is about real people (family, partners, friends), use only the details the musician has shared; when you need more, make them plainly part of the song's world alone.

## Forms that suit story songs

- **Verse-refrain:** verses carry the story, and a repeated line (usually the title) opens or closes each verse. A chorus is optional. The lead sheet's notes or the verses already written show where the refrain sits.
- **AABA:** two verses, a contrasting bridge, and a final verse that returns changed.
- **Verse-chorus with longer verses:** the chorus repeats fewer times and the verses carry more of the song.

## The refrain that changes meaning

This is the craft centre of verse-refrain writing: the refrain's words stay exactly the same, and each verse changes what they mean. Writing backwards from an unchanged refrain is one method: let its setup change the meaning. The musician may instead vary the refrain or write in another order.

**Example (original):** the refrain *I'll be home by nine* (5 syllables).
- Verse 1 ends with the singer at sixteen, shouting it over their shoulder on the way out: a promise they will break.
- Verse 2 ends with the singer years later, saying it on the phone from a late shift: a promise they mean and keep only sometimes.
- Verse 3 ends with the singer's own teenager shouting it on the way out, while the singer, waiting up, finally understands the parent from verse 1.

Same five words, three meanings: carelessness, effort, understanding. The shift explains itself.

## The last verse turns

Save something for the end: a time jump, a reveal, new information, or the narrator's own part in what happened. A final verse that only restates the first wastes the listener's attention at the moment it is highest. When you write a middle verse, leave the turn for the last one.

## Voice

- Conversational and first person is the default here, but character songs in the third person are a long tradition.
- Plain words, natural word order, contractions. Formal or old-fashioned phrasing only if the musician writes that way.
- Humour next to sadness makes both more believable.

## Rhyme

- Lighter than in pop or rap: near rhymes and ABCB schemes are common, and some lines go unrhymed.
- A forced rhyme is especially damaging when every word is exposed. If the honest line misses the rhyme, let it go unrhymed, change the scheme, or re-end the other line.
- Internal rhyme should be subtle and stay in the background.

## Words and melody

- Melodies in this style follow speech rhythm closely, so lines that sound natural spoken will sit well.
- Line lengths can vary within a verse, as long as each new line fits the notes it is sung on.
- Singer-songwriters often vary the melody slightly from verse to verse to fit the words (an added pickup, a split note). Prefer changing words; change notes only when the musician asks or the words are locked.

## Country and Americana leanings

- Titles are often a common phrase that turns: the last verse or chorus gives the phrase a second meaning.
- Stories are concrete and plain-spoken, with the wit carried by the twist and the language kept plain.

## Verify, on top of the lyrics skill's checks

- Every line sounds natural said aloud to the person it is about.
- When an unchanged refrain is intended, keep its words and position; honour a requested variation.
- Consider whether progression, a last-verse turn or deliberate stasis serves this story.
- Review whether implication or an explicit statement best serves the chosen voice.
- Personal details are the musician's own, or plainly part of the song's world.

Example result lines:
- "Verse 3 written: your own kid now says 'I'll be home by nine' · every line fits its notes, refrain word for word, ABCB like verse 1, cliché-free."
- "Verse 2 written: the same promise, now made on the phone from a late shift · every line fits its notes, refrain word for word, the turn left for verse 3."
