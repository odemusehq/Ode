# Singer-songwriter lyrics

With a guitar or piano and one voice, every word is exposed. Listeners lean in to the story and the honesty of the voice, so weak lines have nowhere to hide. The general craft is in the lyrics skill; this file adds what is specific to story-led, acoustic writing.

## Write for exposure

- Every line must stand up when spoken aloud. Read the lyric as if telling it to someone across a table; anything that sounds like filler or decoration will sound worse when sung.
- Understatement often hits harder than declaration. Trust the listener to feel what the details imply.
- Don't spell out the moral at the end. If the story has done its work, the listener already knows.

## Story and detail

- Build scenes from specific, ordinary things: a time of day, the weather, an object on a table, what someone said, a name.
- Let characters act. "She folds his shirts and leaves them on the stairs" says more about a relationship than any adjective.
- Use the details only this narrator would know; they make a song feel lived rather than written.
- Give the story a shape: each verse a scene, time moving forward, and something different by the end.
- When the song is about real people (family, partners, friends), use the details the musician chooses to share, and offer to change identifying ones if they're unsure.

## Forms that suit story songs

- **Verse-refrain:** verses carry the story, and a repeated line (usually the title) opens or closes each verse. There may be no separate chorus.
- **AABA:** two verses, a contrasting bridge, and a final verse that returns changed.
- **Verse-chorus with longer verses:** the chorus repeats fewer times and the verses carry more of the song.

## The refrain that changes meaning

The craft centre of verse-refrain writing: the refrain's words stay the same, but each verse changes what they mean. Design the verses backwards from the refrain so that each one sets it up differently.

**Example (original):** the refrain *I'll be home by nine* (5 syllables).
- Verse 1 ends with the singer at sixteen, shouting it over their shoulder on the way out: a promise they won't keep.
- Verse 2 ends with the singer years later, saying it on the phone from a late shift: a promise they mean and can't always keep.
- Verse 3 ends with the singer's own teenager shouting it on the way out, while the singer, waiting up, finally understands the parent from verse 1.

Same five words, three meanings: carelessness, effort, understanding. No line has to explain it.

## The last verse turns

Save something for the end: a time jump, a reveal, new information, or the narrator's own part in what happened. A final verse that only restates the first wastes the listener's attention at the moment it's highest.

## Voice

- Conversational and first person is the default here, but character songs in the third person are a long tradition.
- Plain words, natural word order, contractions. Formal or old-fashioned phrasing only if the musician writes that way.
- Humour sitting next to sadness makes both more believable.

## Rhyme

- Lighter than in pop or rap: near rhymes and ABCB schemes are common, and some lines go unrhymed.
- A forced rhyme is especially damaging when every word is exposed. If the honest line doesn't rhyme, let it go unrhymed, or re-end the other line.
- Internal rhyme should be subtle; it shouldn't call attention to itself.

## Words and melody

- Melodies in this style often follow speech rhythm closely, so write lines that sound natural spoken, and the melody will follow.
- Line lengths can vary within a verse, as long as the matching lines in other verses fit the same melody.
- Singer-songwriters often adjust the melody slightly from verse to verse to fit the words (an added pickup note, a split note). That flexibility is normal in this style, but ask before assuming the musician wants it.

## Country and Americana leanings

- Titles are often a common phrase that turns: the last chorus or verse gives the phrase a second meaning.
- Stories are concrete and plain-spoken, with wit carried by the twist rather than by ornate language.

## Singer-songwriter checks

On top of the lyrics skill's checks:
- Every line would sound natural said aloud to the person it's about.
- The refrain (if there is one) reads differently after each verse.
- The last verse turns or reveals rather than repeating.
- The moral isn't spelled out.
- Personal details are the musician's own, or clearly fictional.
