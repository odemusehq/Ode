# Syllables, stress and sung phrasing

Ask `lyricist` to interpret the exact words and notes in the intended pronunciation. Forward this reference explicitly when needed. Counts and stress are model assessments, not a local dictionary, a deterministic validator or evidence of a heard singer.

## Map a performance, not two totals

Supply the lyric revision, relevant lines, actual MIDI coordinates and their origin, tempo, meter, phrase boundaries, harmony and the singer's known range/delivery. Ask for the words split into intended sung syllables, their lexical/semantic stresses and the note or note run each uses. Include pickups, held vowels, consonant releases, elisions, repeated attacks and rests/breaths where relevant.

One syllable can span several notes; more syllables may require additional rhythmic attacks. Equal note and syllable counts can still produce wrong stress, an unpronounceable transition or an unwanted emphasis. A syllabic setting can use one syllable per note when that is the intended delivery; it is not the default quality test.

The available time depends on tempo and actual duration, not just a beat count. A singer may extend a vowel and place its closing consonant late. Closely spaced clusters or important syllables need enough articulation time. Judge the whole phrase and avoid fixed minimum durations for every syllable.

## Interpret stress

Distinguish a word's normal stress in its intended pronunciation from the emphasis the speaker gives a sentence. A grammatical role can change stress, as in the noun and verb "record". Dialect, sung reduction and the artist's reading can matter; do not turn a generic word list into the authority.

Content words commonly carry meaning; articles and connecting words commonly carry less weight. Check an unusually long/high function word against the intended sentence. Deliberate emphasis can reverse that expectation. Musical weight includes groove, anticipation, length and phrase position as well as beat and pitch.

For structural metre, compare relative stressed lengths and phrase groupings. Pattison's *Writing Better Lyrics*, second edition, chapters 14-16, treats common meter and couplets as ways of establishing and changing expectation. Matching total syllable counts is not their definition, and regular metre is not the goal for every lyric.

## Pronunciation choices

Words such as "every", "family", "fire" and "hour" can support different sung readings. State the specific reading the proposed phrase needs and check that it suits this singer, tempo and context. Do not silently change pronunciation to make a total fit. The artist's actual performance takes priority over an estimated count.

Contractions can change count, stress or register; they do not always reduce syllables. Decide the complete line before and after the change. Keep ordinary spelling unless a written contraction or elision is itself part of the artist's voice.

UK/US settings are useful hints, not an exhaustive description of a singer's accent. Avoid categorical claims that every UK singer drops post-vocalic r or that a particular word has one national count/stress. Use the specified accent and the actual delivery; identify uncertainty rather than forcing a category.

## Repeated melodies and corrections

Read the reference phrase's actual notes, not merely the previous verse's counts. Review new words against the same timing and stresses. An extra weak pickup, melisma or changed articulation may fit; another phrase may need an authorised note change. Preserve locked words/notes and linked copies.

If a mismatch is unintended, choose within scope: revise onset/duration/rest or pitches for a melody edit; wording for a words-only edit; either when authorised. Retain the meaning and the singer's voice. Recheck the full phrase, its neighbouring lines and any repeated setting after the edit.

Use the prosody skill for meaning/contour, structural expectation and detailed singability. Read back the MIDI and audition the guide; only an actual vocal recording can establish the sung pronunciation and articulation.
