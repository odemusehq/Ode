# Lyrics

A good lyric sounds like a person talking, fits its melody, and makes the listener see and feel something on first hearing. Ode helps the musician write their lyric; it does not replace their lyric with its own.

## Principles

- **The musician is the author.** Offer two or three focused options for the lines in question rather than rewriting the song. Touch only what they asked about.
- **Locked lines stay exactly as they are.** If a locked line causes a problem (its count, its rhyme), say so and work around it in the lines you are allowed to change.
- **Write in their voice, not yours** (see Learning the musician's voice).
- **Check every line before you show it:** syllable count and stress against the target or the melody, rhyme types, prosody when there is a melody, and originality before a lyric is finalised. Never count syllables or judge rhymes by eye. If a check can't run, say the lines are unchecked; if a word's pronunciation is uncertain, say so.
- **Use their English:** their spelling and pronunciation (UK or US), their words ("flat" or "apartment"), their register.
- **Explain each choice in a sentence.** Go deeper only when asked.
- **When you offer three options, label one clearly as outside their usual style** and say which habit it breaks, so they can stretch if they want to.
- **Write new lines every time.** The examples in this skill and its references only illustrate the craft; never reuse their lines in the musician's song.

## Before you write

Gather what you need from the song and the musician; ask only for what you can't find:

- what the song is about in one sentence, and the title (if there's no clear centre yet, use the idea skill first);
- which section you are writing and what its job is (below);
- the lines around it, and the matching section (verse 1 when you write verse 2);
- the melody, if there is one: syllable count and stress pattern for each line, and where the long and high notes fall (see the syllables and prosody skills);
- the rhyme scheme of the matching section;
- which lines are locked;
- the musician's voice.

## Learning the musician's voice

Read their earlier lyrics and the current draft, and notice:

- **Vocabulary and register:** plain or ornate, slang, swearing, regional words, how formal they are.
- **Images they reach for:** domestic, urban, nature, work, the body; literal or figurative.
- **Sentence shape:** short statements, long run-on lines, questions, lists, dialogue.
- **Point of view** and who "you" usually is.
- **Rhyme habits:** perfect or near, end rhyme only or internal too, dense or sparse.
- **Tone:** earnest, wry, angry, tender, playful.
- **Line length and how much they repeat.**

Write inside those habits by default. The option outside their usual style breaks one habit on purpose; name which one. Never bring back a habit the musician has said they want to drop.

Avoid words that make lyrics sound generic or machine-written unless the musician actually uses them: *echoes, whispers, shadows, shattered, embers, neon, tapestry, symphony, ethereal, ignite, soaring*. Choose the plain, specific word a person would say.

## What each section does

| Section | Its job | How it usually sounds |
|---|---|---|
| Verse | Shows: scene, story, characters, specific detail. Each verse moves forward with new information, time or stakes; verse 2 is not a paraphrase of verse 1. | Conversational, detailed, more words per line than the chorus. |
| Pre-chorus | Lifts: builds tension and sets up the title without saying it. Often moves from detail toward feeling. | Shorter phrases, rising energy, unresolved; it leans into the chorus. |
| Chorus | Sums up: the emotional point and the hook, with the title in its strongest spot. It must make sense after every verse. | Simpler, more general and more repetitive than the verses; open vowels on held notes. |
| Bridge | Contrasts: a new angle such as a realisation, confession, time jump, the other person's view or a change of tense. It should make the last chorus mean more. | New line lengths, rhyme scheme and melody. |
| Refrain | In songs without a separate chorus, a repeated line (usually the title) that opens or closes each verse and gathers its meaning. | The same words, read differently after each verse. |

Post-choruses, tags (a repeated last line), intros and outros carry short repeated hooks or echoes; the pop sub-skill covers post-choruses.

## Show, don't tell

Telling names a feeling. Showing gives the listener the evidence and lets them feel it for themselves, which is why it lands harder. Verses should mostly show. Choruses may tell: after the verses have shown, a plain statement feels earned.

> Telling: *I'm so lonely since you left / I miss you every single day*
>
> Showing: *Two mugs on the drainer, I only rinse one / I still buy your oat milk and let it go sour*

The second version never says "lonely", yet the listener sees the habit of two continuing without the other person, which is what loneliness looks like from the inside.

Ways to show: an object, an action or habit, a place at a particular time, a sound or a smell, something the body does, a line of dialogue, or something someone doesn't do any more.

## Concrete imagery and sensory detail

- **Specific nouns beat general ones.** "Car" is weaker than "the hatchback with the cracked wing mirror". Use details that belong to the song's world. When a detail would be a fact about the musician's life, use theirs or ask; don't invent biography.
- **Use more than sight:** sound, smell, taste, touch, temperature, movement, what the body does.
- **Let verbs do the work.** "The door slams" beats "the loud door".
- **One fresh image beats three stock ones.** Don't mix metaphors from different worlds in one line.
- **Anchor abstract words.** Love, pain, soul, forever and heart float away unless a concrete detail sits nearby.
- **Test comparisons for one hearing.** Lyrics are heard once, at tempo. A simile has to be clear immediately and come from the song's world.

## Point of view and tense

- Choose a point of view ("I", "you", "she", "he", "they", "we") and keep it. If "you" starts referring to someone else halfway through, the listener gets lost.
- Choose a tense for each section and keep it. Change it only with a time cue ("back then", "now"), usually at a section boundary.
- Check pronouns and tense on every option you write; they must agree with the lines around them.
- A deliberate switch, such as a bridge that reveals who the song is really about, is powerful once.

## Title placement

- Put the title where the melody and rhythm give it the most weight: the first line of the chorus, the last line, or both. In verse-refrain songs, the first or last line of each verse.
- Make the line before the title set it up, so the title lands as a conclusion or a twist.
- Repeat it often enough for the listener to learn it.
- Don't bury it mid-line, after a long run of words, or on weak beats. Check with the prosody skill that its stressed syllables land on strong beats and that its key vowel can be held.
- Avoid spending the title in the verse or pre-chorus, unless it's a deliberate echo.

## Clichés and filler

**Clichés** are stock phrases and images ("heart of stone", "tears like rain"). They save the writer effort and cost the listener interest. The originality skill covers spotting and freshening them.

**Filler** is there for the count, not the meaning:
- padding words: *just, really, so, very, oh, baby, yeah* when they add nothing;
- an unneeded auxiliary: "I do believe", "you did leave";
- inverted word order to reach a rhyme: "to town we went";
- saying the same thing twice: "cold and freezing";
- a vague "it" with nothing for it to point to.

Fix filler by spending the same syllables on meaning: a detail, a stronger verb, a name, a time of day.

## Line length and symmetry

- **Matching sections need matching lines.** Verse 2 line 1 should have the same syllable count and stress pattern as verse 1 line 1, so it sings to the same melody. Small differences can be absorbed by splitting or tying notes, but ask before changing the melody.
- **Within a section,** equal line lengths feel steady, a shorter line punches, and lengthening lines build. Use the difference on purpose (the prosody skill covers how structure carries feeling).
- **Keep lines singable in one breath** at the song's tempo, or give long lines a natural pause.
- **End lines on the important word.** The end of a line is where the ear lands and usually where the rhyme sits.

## Conversational phrasing

- Write what a person would say, in normal word order. Read the line aloud at speaking speed; if nobody would say it that way, rephrase it, unless the musician deliberately writes in a formal or poetic style.
- Use the contractions the musician uses.
- Natural speech already has rhythm, and keeping it helps stresses land correctly when the line is sung.
- Questions, direct address and dialogue make lyrics feel alive.

## Rewriting techniques

Decide what is wrong before changing anything: the meaning, the image, the sound, the fit with the melody, or the rhyme. Fix that, and keep everything else.

1. **Name the line's job** (what it must show or say) and keep the words that already do it.
2. **Swap an abstract word for a concrete detail.**
3. **Find a stronger verb.**
4. **Cut filler** and spend the space on meaning.
5. **Reorder** so the key word lands at the end of the line.
6. **Change the rhyme sound**, for instance by re-ending the other line of the pair so the rhyme has better options (see the rhymes skill).
7. **Change the point of view, tense or addressee** for a new angle.
8. **Use a different sense.**
9. **Say the opposite, or understate.**
10. **Keep the fit:** match the count and stress pattern of the line you're replacing, or the melody.

### Worked example

The musician's verse 1, line 3 (locked): *The fridge door hums, the window shakes* — 8 syllables, stressed on FRIDGE, HUMS, WIN-, SHAKES (the even syllables), ending on a stressed syllable.

Their verse 2, line 3, which "feels flat": *I'm feeling lonely without you* — also 8 syllables, but stressed on FEEL-, LONE- and -OUT, so the last stress comes a syllable early and the line ends on a weak syllable where verse 1 ends on a strong one. It also tells the feeling instead of showing it. In this ABCB verse, line 3 doesn't need to rhyme.

Options presented to the musician:

1. *Your mug still hangs above the sink* — 8 syllables, stresses match verse 1
   Shows the absence through an object they see every day.
2. *I cook too much and freeze the rest* — 8 syllables, stresses match verse 1
   A habit of cooking for two that hasn't stopped; it lets the listener work out why.
3. *Who gets your Sunday mornings now?* — 8 syllables, stresses match verse 1 *(outside your usual style: a direct question to them instead of an image)*
   More confrontational; it turns the verse toward the other person.

The count already matched, so the fix was the stress pattern and the telling. A matching count alone is not a fit. More examples sorted by problem (telling, clichés, forced rhymes, a buried title, a verse 2 that repeats verse 1) are in `references/rewriting-examples.md`.

## Giving options

- Show only the lines that change, with the line before and after so the musician can read them in place.
- Number the options; give each its syllable count as checked (and the rhyme type with its partner line, if relevant) and one sentence on why.
- Keep the musician's best words in every option where possible.
- Offer two or three; with three, label the one outside their usual style.

## Giving feedback on a draft

- **Start with what works, specifically:** a line, an image, a rhyme. It tells the musician what to protect.
- **Name the two to four changes that would help most,** in order of impact. Quote the line and give the reason in plain words ("verse 2 repeats what verse 1 told us").
- **Offer to draft options** for any of them; don't rewrite unasked.
- **Separate craft from taste.** Say when something is a convention rather than a rule; plenty of great songs break each of these guidelines on purpose.

## Before you present

- Syllable counts and stress fit the target or the melody and match the corresponding lines in the other verses.
- Rhymes are labelled as the check reports them, in the musician's accent.
- With a melody: stressed syllables fall on strong beats, and held notes carry open vowels.
- Point of view, tense and "you" agree with the surrounding lines.
- The title sits in a strong position and isn't spent early.
- No clichés, filler or forced word order; the originality check has run before the lyric is finalised.
- Spelling follows the musician's setting; locked lines are untouched; uncertain pronunciations are marked.
