# Developing a lyric idea

Use the configured `lyricist` for proposed words and interpretation. Forward this reference explicitly when useful. Begin with the musician's intention and actual material; unknown biography stays unknown. Genre and memory can suggest possibilities, not prescribe a writing method.

These techniques draw on Pat Pattison's *Writing Better Lyrics*, second edition, chapters 1-9 and 24. Use the relevant technique, not every exercise for every request.

## Find material before demanding polish

A topic names a broad subject; an idea gives it a useful perspective, situation, question, image or complication. A static image, chant or collage can be a deliberate idea too. Do not require a narrative conflict or a bridge before writing can begin.

When the material is generic or the writer is stuck, ask Lyricist to explore sensory associations around something from this song's world: an object, character, place, action or moment. Let associations travel beyond the first object. Sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, inner bodily feeling and movement are possible routes, not a seven-item quota. Free exploration need not rhyme or resemble finished lyrics.

Choose details that reveal the intended feeling, then try possible lines. Do not assume concrete description always beats a direct statement: an attitude or telling phrase can become the title. Strong verbs and meaningful comparisons can do more than decorative adjectives.

## Develop useful choices

Explore a semantic field alongside several rhyme sounds when a draft feels trapped by its first end word. Ask for candidates that belong to the idea, then compare complete lines. A worksheet expands choice; it is not a fixed list length or a feature the musician must operate.

For metaphor, explore a shared property that makes an unexpected relationship intelligible. Decide whether it is a sustained lens or a passing comparison. Strangeness alone is not success, and a familiar phrase may become expressive through its setup.

Useful questions include who speaks, to whom, why now, what they want, what they notice and what remains unsaid. Choose only the questions that help this material. The narrator can be fictional; do not save their story as the musician's biography.

## Let the idea grow

Ask what each passage adds to the listener's understanding. A later verse can deepen the same moment, change its implications or reveal a stake without advancing time. If two verses merely paraphrase each other, find a new level of understanding or consider distilling/reordering them.

Read verses together without the chorus to check continuity. Then inspect each return into the chorus: does the preceding line make the repeated hook mean something new? An unchanged chorus can gather emotional force; changing a word at every return is not required.

These developing levels are independent of song form. They do not automatically become verse 1, verse 2 and bridge. Consider the form that carries the actual material, preserving necessary scene, cause and transition when moving or deleting a passage.

## Title, hook and perspective

A title can focus the idea, pose a question or gain meaning during the song. It may emerge before, during or after drafting; do not force every starting point into a title-first method. Where a memorable sung title is wanted, test its rhythm and meaning in the actual melodic setting rather than imposing a word-count or vowel rule.

A hook can be lyrical, melodic, rhythmic or instrumental. Repetition builds recognition; context can renew the repeated material. Avoid lifting distinctive wording or melodies from references, but do not claim a model can certify originality.

Choose perspective by the desired distance and speaker motivation. Direct I-to-you address, first-person narration, third-person narrative, narrative-you and self-address offer different relationships. Check why the speaker tells the story and what the listener needs to infer. Quoted dialogue and deliberate time/POV changes need intelligibility, not a single permitted switch.

Keep the useful idea and approved material in the existing lead sheet. Read `lyrics/references/writing.md` for development, power positions, perspective and rewriting; use `lyrics/prosody` for the words' actual musical setting.
