# Hip-hop lyrics

In rap the words are the lead instrument. Rhythm, rhyme and meaning happen at once, and listeners judge both the craft and whether it rings true. The lyrics skill covers the loop and the general craft, and rhyme technique for rap is in `rhymes/rap/`; this file offers approaches to writing bars. Bar groupings, density and breath figures are starting points; the artist's flow can depart from them.

## Bars and sections

- A bar is one measure of the beat (four beats in 4/4). In the lyrics, a rap section's lines are its bars: one line per bar, unless the musician writes lines that run across bar lines; count bars against the beat, whatever the layout of lines on the page.
- Verses are commonly 16 bars, with 8, 12, 24 and 32 also common. Hooks are commonly 8 bars, often a 4-bar phrase repeated or varied.
- Think in 4-bar units. Ideas, rhyme sounds and flow patterns often change at 4- or 8-bar points, and punchlines tend to land at the end of a 4-bar unit.
- Write exactly the number of bars the section has (its length in the lead sheet) or the request names; shape the idea to fit those bars, and keep the verse at that length.

## Flow

Flow is the rhythm of the syllables against the beat: where they start, how dense they are, where the pauses fall, and which syllables are punched.

- **Density.** Run Lyricist's syllable assessment on the musician's own bars to learn how many syllables per bar they rap, and stay in that range unless the request asks otherwise. At a given tempo, more syllables per bar feels urgent; fewer feels laid-back and leaves room for emphasis.
- **Grid.** Straight sixteenth notes, triplets (three syllables per beat), double-time (twice the usual density) and half-time feels are all established; know which one the verse uses.
- **Placement.** Stressed syllables and rhymes on the snare (beats 2 and 4) give punch; just behind the beat feels relaxed; starting phrases before the beat pulls forward. Match the musician's habit from their own verses.
- **Variation.** Switch density or pattern at a 4- or 8-bar point to keep attention, then return.
- **Breath.** Plan a breath point every two to four bars. A dense bar needs a short gap or a lighter bar after it. Read every bar out loud at tempo; use that as a prompt to review the singer's actual delivery, not proof of their limits.
- **Tempo.** At slower tempos (around 80 to 95 BPM), dense sixteenth-note bars fit comfortably. At faster tempos felt in half time (around 130 to 150 BPM), bars are often sparser, with triplet runs for contrast.

## Punchlines and wordplay

- **Setup and payoff.** A punchline needs a setup bar that makes a promise and a payoff bar that turns it. Put the payoff word at the end of the bar, where it gets the most weight and the strongest rhyme.
- **Double meanings.** Words and phrases with two meanings (homophones, idioms taken literally, slang with a second sense) reward replays. The meaning has to change, or it's only repetition.
- **Comparisons.** Similes and metaphors should surprise and still be clear at tempo. An extended metaphor carried across several bars shows control; keep its pieces from the same world.
- **References** to brands, places, people and events date quickly and can shut out listeners who are unfamiliar with them. Use references from the musician's own world, and only when they add meaning.
- Every punchline serves the verse's subject. A clever line that changes the topic costs more than it earns.

**Example (original), setup and payoff with a double meaning:**

> *Workin' the till, every shift I'm countin' other people's change*
> *Late nights writin', 'cause I swear I'm gonna be the one who changed*

The setup uses "change" literally (coins); the payoff turns it into transformation. Ending two bars on almost the same word usually sounds lazy; here the shift in meaning is the point, and Lyricist's rhyme assessment calls the pair a near rhyme. About 15 syllables a bar, a dense flow at around 90 BPM.

## Storytelling

- Set scenes with concrete detail: place, time, weather, objects, what people say.
- Let verses jump in time or point of view, and give the last verse a turn: a consequence, a reveal, a change in the narrator.
- Dialogue in quotation marks, with a change in delivery, brings characters to life.
- Third-person stories and characters are as valid as first-person verses; make clear which is which.

## Truth to the artist

Rap audiences hear false claims instantly, and a lyric in the artist's mouth is a statement about their life.

- Every biographical claim about the musician comes from them: money, crime, violence, drugs, relationships, family, where they grew up, what they've survived. Use only what they've told you, what Ode remembers them saying, or what's in their own lyrics. When a bar needs a fact beyond what they've shared, build it from the song's own world.
- Infer lifestyle, background and experiences only from what the musician has shared; their name, ethnicity, nationality, gender and genre stay out of it. Genre stereotypes (flexing, street clichés) sound fake unless the musician writes that way.
- Use the musician's own vocabulary and slang. UK and US rap differ in words and pronunciation; add only slang they use.
- Follow the musician's own choices on explicit language: write clean when their lyrics are clean.

## Hooks and ad-libs

- A rap hook is usually simpler and more repetitive than the verses; it can be sung, chanted or rapped, and it normally carries the title.
- Ad-libs and call-and-response go in brackets in the line. They're part of an artist's signature: add them sparingly, and write only original ones: another artist's recognisable ad-libs and catchphrases stay with that artist.

## Verify, on top of the lyrics skill's checks

- The bar count is exactly the section's length or the number asked for.
- Lyricist's syllable assessment per bar shows a density in the musician's range (or the requested change), with breath points.
- Review punchline placement and relevance against the intended flow; a payoff may land inside or across a bar.
- Rhymes are labelled as Lyricist's rhyme assessment labels them, with a delivery note where they depend on it.
- Every claim about the musician's life is one they gave you, and everything is original.

Example result lines:
- "Verse 2 written, 16 bars: the same shift seen from the manager's side · 13–16 syllables a bar like verse 1, end rhymes switch every 4 bars, a breath every 4 bars."
- "Hook tightened to 8 bars, two 4-bar halves with the title closing each · 9–11 syllables a bar, all original, clean like your verses."
