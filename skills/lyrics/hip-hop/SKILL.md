# Hip-hop lyrics

In rap the words are the lead instrument. Rhythm, rhyme and meaning all happen at once, and listeners judge both the craft and whether it rings true. The general craft is in the lyrics skill and rhyme technique for rap is in the rhymes skill (`rhymes/rap/`); this file adds what is specific to writing bars.

## Bars and sections

- A bar is one measure of the beat (usually four beats in 4/4). A rhymed line usually fills one bar, but lines can run across bar lines; count bars against the beat, not lines on the page.
- Verses are commonly 16 bars, with 8, 12, 24 and 32 also common. Hooks are commonly 8 bars, often a 4-bar phrase repeated or varied.
- Think in 4-bar units. Ideas, rhyme sounds and flow patterns often change at 4- or 8-bar points, and punchlines tend to land at the end of a 4-bar unit.
- When the musician asks for "8 bars", give exactly 8; number the bars so they can check.

## Flow

Flow is the rhythm of the syllables against the beat: where they start, how dense they are, where the pauses fall, and which syllables are punched.

- **Density.** Count syllables per bar in the musician's own verses and stay in that range unless they ask otherwise. At a given tempo, more syllables per bar feels urgent; fewer feels laid-back and leaves room for emphasis.
- **Grid.** Straight sixteenth notes, triplets (three syllables per beat, a triplet flow), double-time (twice the usual density) and half-time feels are all established; name the one you mean.
- **Placement.** Landing stressed syllables and rhymes on the snare (beats 2 and 4) gives punch; landing them just behind the beat feels relaxed; starting phrases before the beat pulls forward. Match the musician's habit, which you can hear in their past verses or ask about.
- **Variation.** Switch density or pattern at a 4- or 8-bar point to keep attention, then return.
- **Breath.** Plan a breath point every two to four bars. A dense bar needs a short gap or a lighter bar after it. Read every bar out loud at tempo; if you run out of air, the musician will too.
- **Tempo.** At slower tempos (around 80 to 95 BPM), dense sixteenth-note bars fit comfortably. At faster tempos felt in half time (around 130 to 150 BPM), lines are often sparser, with triplet runs for contrast.

## Punchlines and wordplay

- **Setup and payoff.** A punchline needs a setup bar that makes a promise and a payoff bar that turns it. Put the payoff word at the end of the bar, where it gets the most weight and the strongest rhyme.
- **Double meanings.** Words and phrases with two meanings (homophones, idioms taken literally, slang with a second sense) reward replays. The meaning has to change, or it's only repetition.
- **Comparisons.** Similes and metaphors should be surprising but instantly clear at tempo. An extended metaphor carried across several bars shows control; keep its pieces from the same world.
- **References** to brands, places, people and events date quickly and can alienate listeners who don't know them. Use references from the musician's own world, and only when they add meaning.
- Every punchline still serves the verse's subject. A clever line that changes the topic costs more than it earns.

**Example (original), setup and payoff with a double meaning:**

> *Workin' the till, every shift I'm countin' other people's change*
> *Late nights writin', 'cause I swear I'm gonna be the one who changed*

The setup puts "change" in its literal sense (coins); the payoff turns it into transformation. Ending two bars on almost the same word usually sounds lazy; here the shift in meaning makes the repeat the point. Each bar has about 15 syllables, a dense flow at around 90 BPM.

## Storytelling

- Set scenes with concrete detail: place, time, weather, objects, what people say.
- Let verses jump in time or point of view, and give the last verse a turn: a consequence, a reveal, a change in the narrator.
- Dialogue in quotation marks, with a change in delivery, brings characters to life.
- Third-person stories and characters are as valid as first-person verses; make clear which is which.

## Truth to the artist

Rap audiences hear false claims instantly, and a lyric in the artist's mouth is a statement about their life.

- Never invent biographical claims for the musician: money, crime, violence, drugs, relationships, family, where they grew up, what they've survived. Use only what they've told you or what's in their own lyrics, and ask before adding anything personal.
- Never infer lifestyle, background or experiences from the musician's name, ethnicity, nationality, gender or genre. Genre stereotypes (flexing, street clichés) sound fake unless the musician writes that way themselves.
- Use the musician's own vocabulary and slang. UK and US rap differ in words and pronunciation; don't add slang they don't use.
- Follow the musician's own choices on explicit language, and ask whether they need a clean version.

## Hooks and ad-libs

- A rap hook is usually simpler and more repetitive than the verses; it can be sung, chanted or rapped, and it normally carries the title.
- Ad-libs and call-and-response go in brackets. They're part of an artist's signature: suggest them sparingly, and never borrow another artist's recognisable ad-libs or catchphrases.

## Presenting bars

- Number the bars.
- Mark the main rhymes (end and internal) as the check reports them, and say which bars carry the punchline.
- Give syllables per bar when density matters, and mark breath points and any delivery notes (a word to stretch, a pause, a double).
- With options, vary the approach rather than the vocabulary: one could change the flow pattern, another the angle on the story.

## Hip-hop checks

On top of the lyrics skill's checks:
- The bar count is exactly what was asked for.
- Density per bar matches the musician's range (or the requested change), with breath points.
- Punchlines land at the end of a bar, and each one serves the subject.
- Nothing claims a fact about the musician's life that they haven't given you.
- No borrowed lines, catchphrases, flows or ad-libs from other artists.
