# Pop lyrics

Pop lyrics aim for instant clarity: a listener understands the chorus on first hearing and sings it by the second. The suggestions below serve that aim when the musician wants it; unusual phrasing, a withheld title or an unresolved chorus remain available. The lyrics skill covers the loop (read, decide, change in place, verify, let them hear it, report in one line) and the general craft; this file adds only what is specific to pop.

## Get to the hook quickly

- Intros are short, and the first chorus usually arrives within about the first minute. Keep verse 1 lean so the listener reaches the hook fast.
- Verse 2 is often shorter than verse 1 in modern pop (for example half the length, straight into the pre-chorus). Shortening a section removes bars the musician wrote, so do it only when asked; when verse 2 is struggling for new material, write a lean verse 2 on the melody that is there.
- Every verse line earns its place: one clear detail per line is plenty.

## The chorus and its title

- The title usually sits in the first line of the chorus, the last line, or both, and is often repeated several times. In pop, repeating the title is the hook working.
- For a sing-back title, try short, rhythmic, with an open vowel on the word that is held.
- The chorus is simpler and more general than the verses: everyday words, short lines, each sentence kept within one line.
- Common chorus shapes, described by what the lines do:
  - title, answer line, title, answer line;
  - three setup lines and the title last, the title line unrhymed so it stands out;
  - the title as a short chant, with one line that changes;
  - lines that start the same way, building to the title.
- In the last chorus, one changed word or line can deepen the meaning so the final repeat lands harder. The unchanged chorus can also deepen through a new verse or lead-in. Respect linked copies, locks and the requested scope before changing an individual repetition.

**Example (original), three setup lines and the title last:**

> *Tell them I'm away* (5)
> *Tell them I'm a ghost today* (7)
> *Tell them try again in May* (7)
> *I'm out of office* (5)

The lines that start the same way build momentum; Lyricist's rhyme assessment (US) calls "away" / "today" / "May" perfect; and the title line breaks the pattern so it sticks.

## Pre-chorus

- Shorter lines and rising pressure, often matched by the production building.
- Move from verse detail toward feeling: a question, a "when" or "if" clause, a realisation that the chorus answers.
- Keep the title out of it so the chorus gets the payoff.
- When anticipation is wanted, delay an expected rhyme or musical resolution, or reshape a phrase. Judge the established grouping; odd line counts and unrhymed endings alone do not guarantee forward motion.

## Post-chorus

- A short repeated hook after the chorus: one to four words, often a fragment of the title, a single word, or a vowel sound.
- Rhythmic and easy to sing; it often works with the production (a vocal chop, a synth line), so write it on the notes that are there.
- Repeating the same word is normal here.

## Words and lines

- Everyday, short words; contractions; normal word order. Avoid obscure references and words most listeners would have to look up.
- Universal and specific at once: a situation any listener can step into, with one or two details that make it unmistakably this song.
- "You" addressed directly is common because it lets listeners put themselves in the song. Give "you" a gender only when the musician has.
- Present tense is common in pop because it feels immediate.
- A twist on an everyday phrase makes a strong pop title.

## Singing at tempo

- At dance tempos, fast syllables need simple consonants; save consonant clusters for slower moments.
- Leave rests. A chorus packed wall to wall with syllables leaves the hook without room to breathe.
- Put long notes on open vowels, above all the title's key word.

## Writing to a track or topline

Pop toplines often come before the words, sung with placeholder syllables or half-words. When the melody is there:

- Read the exact notes and ask Lyricist for an intended sung mapping, including stresses, melismas, articulation and breaths. Do not infer syllable count from note count; preserve a locked melody.
- Consider keeping a placeholder's vowel on long notes when that sound is part of what the musician wants to retain. If they sang "oh" on the long note, look for a word with that vowel there; it's the sound they fell for.
- Keep the placeholder's rhythm, including phrases that start before or after the beat.
- If a placeholder phrase already sounds like real words, it may be the title: use it, and say so in the result line.

## Verify, on top of the lyrics skill's checks

- When immediate clarity is the aim, review the chorus and title placement for it; an indirect or unrepeated title may be deliberate.
- Review a post-chorus hook against the requested rhythm and length; one to four words is a starting point.
- Inspect singability findings at tempo with this singer's delivery and breaths; keep intentional vowel colour or dense phrasing.
- Verse 2 brings something new beyond verse 1.

Example result lines:
- "Chorus rebuilt around the title: 'Out of Office' closes it after three 'Tell them' lines · 5–7 syllables a line on the notes, 'away' / 'today' / 'May' perfect (US)."
- "Post-chorus written: 'Out, out, out of office' on the vocal-chop figure · 6 syllables on its 6 notes, open 'ow' on the long notes."
