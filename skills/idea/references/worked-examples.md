# Worked examples: finding the centre

Three examples of the idea loop: what Ode reads, what it decides, what it writes into the lead sheet, what it checks and the one line it replies with. All titles and lyrics here are original and only illustrate the method; never reuse them in a real song.

## Contents
1. A bright loop and a goodbye
2. A draft with no centre
3. A title the musician typed

## 1. A bright loop and a goodbye

**What is there.** A 128 BPM major-key piano loop and a beat; the lead sheet is empty. Ode remembers the musician saying, last session, that their grandmother's house is being sold, and that their songs are usually first-person and reflective. The musician taps "Find a title".

**What to notice.** The music sounds happy and the subject is a goodbye. That clash is an opportunity, not a problem: joyful music under a goodbye often makes the loss hit harder, because the listener feels the life that was in the house. Don't slow the loop down or move it to a minor key; use the energy.

**What Ode writes into the lead sheet:**
- **Title:** Every Room Was Loud
- **Theme:** gratitude for a house full of family life, with the grief waiting underneath.
- **Story:** the last weekend before the sale, the family clearing rooms together. Verse 1: the noise of family gatherings. Verse 2: boxes, and what turns up in each room. Bridge: the first quiet the house has ever had. Last chorus: "every room was loud" has become a memory instead of a description.
- **Point of view:** first person; past tense in the verses, present in the chorus.
- **Mood:** busy, warm, bittersweet.
- **Hook:** Every room was loud.

**Checks.** The syllable check gives 5|6 ("every" is 2 or 3); the stresses fall on EV-, ROOM and LOUD, and "loud" holds the open "ow" vowel. The originality check flags nothing.

**Result line:** "Title: 'Every Room Was Loud': the family clearing your grandmother's house, with the bright loop as the noise it used to hold · 5|6 syllables ('every' 2|3), open 'ow' on 'loud', no clichés."

**On Again**, a clearly different take: *I'll Keep the Marks* (4 syllables), where the house itself sings and promises to keep the pencil marks of the children's heights on the door frame. It breaks the musician's first-person habit, so the result line ends "· outside your usual style: the house sings, not you."

## 2. A draft with no centre

**What is there.** The lead sheet holds the musician's draft and no title:

> *Verse 1*
> Everything is changing fast
> Nothing good is meant to last
> I keep thinking about you
> Don't know what I'm meant to do
>
> *Chorus*
> Hold on, hold on, we'll find a way
> Tomorrow is another day
>
> *Verse 2*
> I drove past your parents' place on Sunday
> The swing's still hanging from the apple tree
> I sat there with the engine running
> Didn't know if you'd want to see me

The musician taps "Find a title".

**What to notice.**
- Verse 1 and the chorus are general statements and stock phrases; they could belong to any song. The originality check also flags "way" / "day", an over-used pair.
- Verse 2 is alive: a place, a day, an object, an action, and a choice the singer can't make. The song's real centre is sitting outside someone's house, unable to decide whether to knock.
- The title is already in the draft: *Engine Running*. It holds the situation (parked outside), the feeling (ready to leave at any moment) and the stakes (go in, or drive away).
- Don't assume who "you" is (an ex, a friend, a sibling). The draft doesn't say, and the song can work for all three.

**What Ode writes into the lead sheet:**
- **Title:** Engine Running
- **Theme:** wanting to reach out and fearing the answer.
- **Story:** parked outside the parents' house on a Sunday, engine on. Verse 1: what happened before that Sunday. Verse 2: the swing, the engine, the choice. Bridge: what the singer would say if the door opened. Last chorus: still running, but now as a decision not taken.
- **Point of view:** first person, present tense, to "you" (who "you" is stays open).
- **Mood:** restless, tender.
- **Hook:** I left the engine running.

The lyric lines stay as the musician wrote them; rewriting verse 1 and the chorus around the new centre is lyric work, for when they ask.

**Checks.** "Engine Running" is 4 syllables, stressed on EN- and RUN-; the hook line *I left the engine running* is 7. The originality check flags nothing in either.

**Result line:** "Title: 'Engine Running', from your verse 2: parked outside their parents' place, not sure whether to knock · 4 syllables, no clichés; verse 1 and the chorus tell what verse 2 shows, and 'way' / 'day' is an over-used pair."

## 3. A title the musician typed

**What is there.** An empty lead sheet and a chord loop. The musician types "half the rent".

**What Ode does.** A short phrase with nothing selected and no song yet reads as a title. Ode keeps the musician's words as the title and answers the title's questions for itself:
- What does it mean, literally and otherwise? Paying half the rent on a shared flat; and a life that was shared in halves.
- Who says it, and to whom? The one who stayed, to the one who moved out.
- Why now? The first rent day after the move.
- What could make it mean something different by the last chorus? It was never about the money.

Each answer gets a home in the story: verse 1 the first rent day alone; verse 2 strangers viewing the spare room; the bridge, the admission that the money is the easy part; the last chorus, half the rent as half a life.

**What Ode writes into the lead sheet:** the title *Half the Rent*; the theme (practical worries standing in for grief); the story as above; the point of view (first person, present tense, to "you", ungendered); the mood (wry, tired, tender); the hook *You left me half the rent*.

**Checks.** The title is 3 syllables, stressed on HALF and RENT; the hook is 6, stressed on LEFT, HALF and RENT. The originality check flags nothing.

**Result line:** "Your title 'Half the Rent' now has a song: the first rent day after a flatmate moves out, told wry · hook 'You left me half the rent', 6 syllables, stress on LEFT, HALF and RENT, no clichés."
