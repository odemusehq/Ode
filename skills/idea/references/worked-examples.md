# Worked examples: shaping ideas

Two more examples in the same shape as the one in SKILL.md. All lyrics here are original and only illustrate the method; never reuse them as material for a real song.

## 1. Starting from a loop that "doesn't match" the subject

**What the musician brought:** *"Bouncy major-key piano loop at 128 BPM. I love it, but it sounds too happy for what I want to write about: my grandmother's house is being sold."*

**What to notice.** The musician thinks the music and the subject clash. That clash is an opportunity, not a problem: joyful music under a goodbye often makes the loss hit harder, because the listener feels the life that was in the house. Don't suggest slowing the loop down or moving it to a minor key unless they ask; offer directions that use the energy.

1. Feeling: gratitude for a house full of family life, with grief waiting underneath.
2. Situation: the last weekend before the sale; the family clearing rooms together.
3. Point of view: open. First person is the natural choice, but this starting point also suits an unusual one (see direction 3).
4. Title candidates: phrases that hold both the joy and the ending.
5. One sentence: a family clears out a loud, much-loved house, and the singer realises the noise was the home.

**Directions as presented:**

**1. Every Room Was Loud** — the loop's energy becomes the noise of family gatherings; the loss is named only in the bridge.
- Core: gratitude that outweighs the grief, for now. First person, past tense in the verses, present in the chorus.
- Hook line: *Every room was loud* (5|6 syllables; "every" can be sung as 2 or 3)

**2. Boxes in the Hall** — a list song about clearing the house and what turns up in each room.
- Core: busy hands keeping grief at arm's length. First person, present tense.
- Hook line: *Boxes in the hall* (5 syllables)

**3. I'll Keep the Marks** *(outside your usual style: the house sings, not a person; your songs are usually first-person confessions)* — the house says goodbye to the family and promises to keep the pencil marks of their heights on the door frame.
- Core: tenderness and letting go, from the side that stays behind.
- Hook line: *I'll keep the marks on the door frame* (8 syllables)

Which way feels right, or would you rather the bright loop carry something heavier?

## 2. Rescuing a draft with no centre

**The musician's draft:**

> *Verse 1*
> Everything is changing fast
> Nothing good is meant to last
> I keep thinking about you
> Don't know what I'm meant to do
>
> *Chorus*
> Hold on, hold on, we'll find a way
> Tomorrow is another day
>
> *Verse 2*
> I drove past your parents' place on Sunday
> The swing's still hanging from the apple tree
> I sat there with the engine running
> Didn't know if you'd want to see me

**Diagnosis.**
- Verse 1 and the chorus are general statements and stock phrases ("nothing good is meant to last", "tomorrow is another day"). They could belong to any song.
- Verse 2 is alive: a place, a day, an object, an action, and a choice the singer can't make. The song's real centre is sitting outside someone's house, unable to decide whether to knock.
- The title candidate is already in the draft: *Engine Running*. It holds the situation (parked outside), the feeling (ready to leave at any moment) and the stakes (go in, or drive away).

**How to present it.** Lead with what works and keep the musician's lines:

> Verse 2 is the heart of this song: the swing, the engine running, not knowing if they'd want to see you. I'd build the whole song around that moment. "Engine Running" could be the title.
>
> Keep: all of verse 2, almost as it is.
> Rethink: verse 1 and the chorus. They tell us you're unsettled; verse 2 already shows it. Verse 1 could show what happened before Sunday, and the chorus could be the choice you're stuck on.

Then offer directions:

**1. Engine Running** — the whole song takes place in the parked car outside their parents' place.
- Core: wanting to reach out and fearing the answer. First person to "you", present tense.
- Hook line: *I left the engine running* (7 syllables)

**2. The Swing's Still Up** — what hasn't changed (the swing, the street) against what has (the two of you).
- Core: comfort and loss in the same picture. First person, present tense.
- Hook line: *The swing's still up* (4 syllables)

Ask one question only if it changes the direction: "Do they come out of the house in the end, or do you drive away?" The answer decides whether the last chorus resolves or stays open.

Don't assume who "you" is (an ex, a friend, a sibling). The draft doesn't say, and the song can work for all three.
