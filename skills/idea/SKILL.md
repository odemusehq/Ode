# Song ideas

An idea is more than a topic. "Heartbreak" is a topic. "Learning to cook for one after years of cooking for two" is an idea: a situation with a feeling inside it, and a phrase that could be its title. In Ode the idea lives in the lead sheet, as the title and what the song is about, where every later piece of work on the song reads it. Find the centre, write it there, check the title and hook, and say it in one line.

## The loop

1. **Read.**
   - The lead sheet (the title, what the song is about, what is locked) and the lyrics, every section's lines under its label.
   - The project map: tempo, key, which parts exist (a loop, a beat, a hummed melody), what is selected, and the hand edits since the last request, which show what the musician was just working on.
   - What Ode remembers about the artist: the themes, points of view and moods they usually write, the images they reach for, what they dislike, and situations from their life they have told you about.
2. **Decide** with the craft below. Start from what the musician brought: their own phrases first (a striking line in a draft, a word in a hum), then the music, then what they have told you. Choose the one strongest direction, and only that one. Again gives another take, and the version history keeps this one.
3. **Change in place.** Write into the lead sheet, replacing what was there:
   - **title:** one to five words;
   - **theme:** what the song means, with the feeling and its complication ("relieved it's over, and guilty about the relief");
   - **story:** the situation (who, where, when, what just happened, what the singer wants) and where it goes: a phrase each for verse 1, verse 2 and the bridge, and how the last chorus reads differently;
   - **point of view:** the person and tense, and who "you" is, without giving anyone a gender or identity beyond what the musician has given;
   - **mood:** in the musician's own terms, kept independent of the key;
   - **hook:** the hook line, usually the title line.
   Idea work changes only the title and what the song is about; the lyrics and the Set stay as they are. Use a title the lyric already sings where you can; writing lines around a new title is lyric work, done when the musician asks ("Write the chorus", "Put the title in the chorus").
4. **Verify.**
   - **Syllable and stress check** on the title and the hook line: know the count and where the stresses fall. Good: the stresses fall on the important words, and the word most likely to be held has an open vowel ("go", "down", "stay", "far").
   - **Originality check** on the title and the hook line: clear of clichés and over-used pairs. A stock phrase, or the title of a very famous song, gets replaced, unless it is the musician's own choice: then keep it and say so in the result line.
   - **Prosody check**, when the title is already sung on notes: its stressed syllables on strong beats.
   - A word the check marks as uncertain (a name, slang) stays out of what you report as checked.
   Fix and check again, up to three rounds; if something still fails, the result line says what.
5. **Let them hear it.** A title and what the song is about live in the lead sheet, so the result line is the whole answer. If you also changed lines because the musician asked, the result line names their section, so the musician can play it from the locator named like its label.
6. **Report in one line**: the title and the song in a few words · what was checked. For example:
   - "Title: 'Spare Room': putting a friend up after their break-up, told by the host · 2 syllables, stress on SPARE and ROOM, cliché-free."
   - "Song is now about the late shift at a launderette, told by the attendant · title 'Not in Service', 4 syllables, cliché-free · outside your usual style: a character's voice."
   - "Title kept, 'Paper Crown' is your phrase · what it's about now written: the kid who is king for one birthday, told years later · 3 syllables, stress on PA- and CROWN, cliché-free."

## Asking

Idea work rarely needs a question. Skip the interview: choose a direction and let the musician react with Undo, Again or a few words. A title the musician already chose is theirs; replace it only when they ask for a new one. In Manual, Ode shows the change for Apply or Skip, and you work the same way.

## What makes an idea strong

Test every direction against these. They explain why some ideas write themselves and others stall at verse two.

1. **One emotional core.** Name the feeling precisely, ideally with its complication: "relieved it's over and guilty about the relief" says far more than "sad". A precise feeling tells you which details to choose and what the chorus has to say.
2. **A specific situation.** Who, where, when, what just happened. Specific situations make universal feelings believable; general statements make them forgettable.
3. **Something at stake.** A want, a choice, a conflict or a question the song circles. Without it, the verses turn into a list of moods.
4. **Somewhere to go.** Verse 2 can add something verse 1 left out (time passes, stakes rise, a reveal, a wider view), and the bridge can shift the angle. If only verse 1 comes to mind, the idea is a single moment; that can be right for a short, hook-driven track, but know it before writing.
5. **A title that carries it** (see Titles).
6. **A fresh angle.** Familiar feelings are fine; the telling has to be fresh. Look for the less obvious side: the one who left, where most songs take the one left behind; the morning after, where most take the night; gratitude inside a break-up; humour inside grief; an object's point of view.

## Where ideas come from

When the musician arrives empty-handed, look here, starting with what they have told you about their life:

- an everyday phrase that can mean two things, given a literal or personal twist;
- an object that holds a story (a spare key, a borrowed coat, a voicemail still saved);
- the moment just before or just after something big;
- a question the singer is unable to answer;
- a contradiction ("I miss the arguments");
- a list (things left behind in a flat, rules of a friendship);
- a place at a specific time of day;
- an instruction or plea to someone ("Stay…", "Tell me…");
- something said in a real conversation the musician remembers (their own, clear of famous quotations).

## From a phrase or a feeling to the lead sheet

Work through these quickly; each step narrows the next, and each fills a field.

1. **Name the feeling and its complication** → theme.
2. **Find the situation:** who is singing, to whom, where, when, what just happened, what the singer wants → story.
3. **Choose the point of view and tense** → point of view.
4. **Find the title:** the phrase that holds the feeling and the situation, often already in the musician's words → title.
5. **Say the song in one sentence.** If it needs two sentences, there may be two songs.
6. **Sketch the journey:** a phrase each for verse 1, verse 2 and the bridge, and how the chorus reads differently by the end → the rest of the story.

## Titles

A title is a promise: the chorus keeps it and the verses earn it. Starting from a title gives every line a target.

**What makes a good title:**
- Short and easy to say: one to five words is typical.
- Concrete or striking: an image, a twisted everyday phrase, or something a person would actually say.
- It carries the idea: it makes sense on its own and means more once the song has been heard.
- Singable: its stressed syllables fall on its important words, and the word most likely to be held has an open vowel.
- Still interesting the fourth time it is sung.
- Fresh: clear of stock phrases and of the titles of very famous songs; a famous namesake invites comparison and makes the song harder to find.

**Ask the title questions, and answer them yourself.** What does it mean, literally and otherwise? Who says it, and to whom? Why now? What happened before, and what happens next? How does the singer feel about it? What could make it mean something different by the last chorus?

**Give each answer a home in the story:** verse 1 answers one question (usually the situation), verse 2 another (the consequences, or new information), the bridge the deepest one (the realisation or the confession), and the chorus states the title with the core feeling. Then each section has a job before a line is written.

**Where the title sits:** the first line of the chorus, the last line, or both; in verse-refrain songs, the first or last line of each verse. The lyrics skill covers placement.

## Hooks

A hook is whatever the listener remembers and sings back: usually the title line, sometimes a melodic or rhythmic figure, a riff, or a short chant after the chorus. A good hook line:

- is short, with a clear natural rhythm when spoken;
- sounds like something a person could say;
- carries a twist, a question or an image;
- survives repetition;
- leaves room for the melody (fewer, longer syllables are easier to make catchy).

The melodic side of the hook belongs to the melody skill.

## Point of view and tense

| Point of view | What it gives | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| First person ("I") | confession, immediacy, inner feeling | a list of feelings without a situation; anchor it in detail |
| Second person ("you") | intimacy, accusation, advice, a message to someone | make it clear who "you" is, and keep it the same person |
| Third person (a name, "she", "he", "they") | story, character, a little distance | listeners may feel less; a first-person chorus or a final reveal brings them in |
| "We" | shared experience, community, anthems | can get vague; ground it in one shared moment |

Switching point of view works best once and on purpose, for example third-person verses whose bridge reveals that the character is the singer.

Tense sets the distance. Present tense feels like it is happening now; past tense feels like memory or a story being told; future and "would" feel like hope, dread or promises. Keep the tense steady within a section, and mark a shift with a time cue ("back then", "now").

## Theme and story shapes

The theme is what the song means; the story is what happens. Choose the shape that suits the idea:

- **Tell a story:** each verse is a scene, time moves forward, the chorus comments.
- **Stay in one moment:** the same moment, seen more deeply in each verse.
- **State a feeling and prove it:** the chorus states it, the verses give the evidence.
- **Build a list:** the items build to a point, and the last one lands hardest.
- **Speak to someone directly:** the song is a letter, a voicemail or a toast; the form sets the tone.

## Starting from music

When the starting point is a loop, a beat, a chord progression or a hummed melody:

- **Read mood from the whole track, with key and mode as loose hints:** a minor key can sound happy and a major key can sound sad. Tempo, groove, sound and what the musician has said about the track matter more.
- **Contrast is a direction:** bittersweet words over bright music, or calm words over a restless beat, is an established and powerful device.
- **Listen to placeholder words.** A hummed or mumbled melody often carries vowels and rhythms that suggest a title; look for a real phrase with the same vowels and stresses.
- **Let tempo and density guide you.** Slow songs carry fewer words and longer vowels; fast songs suit more words and short, punchy phrases.

## When a draft has lost its centre

Signs: verses that could swap places; a chorus that says something different from the verses; a title that appears once; lines that are all feeling without a situation.

Find the most alive line in the draft, often a specific image buried in verse 2, and make it the centre: its phrase becomes the title, its situation the story. Keep the musician's lines; rewriting the sections around the new centre is lyric work, for when they ask. The result line can point to the lines that tell the feeling outright, so the musician knows where to go next.

## Without bias, without copying

- Take everything about the musician from their own words, their songs and what Ode remembers them saying alone; their name, gender, age, nationality and genre stay out of it.
- Write inside their usual themes and points of view by default. When a direction is outside their usual style (a different point of view, mood or form), the result line says so.
- Write new material every time. The examples here and in the references only illustrate the method; keep their titles and lines out of real songs.
