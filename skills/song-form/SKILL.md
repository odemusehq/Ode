# Song form

Song form decides when the listener hears what: when the story starts, when the hook arrives, when the song rests and when it peaks. Good form balances repetition, which builds familiarity, with contrast, which holds attention. This skill owns the plan — the sections, their lengths and order, the locators that mark them, and the lead sheet's list of sections. Which parts play in each section, builds, breakdowns, transitions and moving clips belong to the arrangement skill.

## How Ode works on form

### 1. Read

- From the project map: tempo, meter, the locators (names and times), and where each track's clips start and end in the Arrangement. A section usually begins where several parts change at once.
- Clip names, the lead sheet's sections (kind, name, start beat, length, chords) and the lyrics, whose labels name the sections that have words: what each section is and what it says.
- Genre and intended use from the song's settings and memory: a streaming single, a DJ track, a live set, a sync brief.
- What is locked.

### 2. Decide

- Name each section by its job (the table below), and number repeats in order.
- Use phrase lengths that serve the material. Four- or eight-bar groups are useful starting points, not required units; retain uneven or sub-bar sections when intended.
- Give each section an energy level and know what changes between neighbours.
- When the request changes lengths or order, decide them here; the arrangement skill moves the parts to match.

### 3. Change it in place

- **Locators.** One at the actual start of each section, normally its first downbeat, named consistently with the song and numbered where useful: "Intro", "Verse 1", "Pre-Chorus 1", "Chorus 1", "Post-Chorus 1", "Bridge", "Breakdown", "Build", "Drop 1", "Outro". For a pickup, an upbeat entry or an irregular section, use the intended boundary rather than snapping the music or locator to a whole bar. Add a key or tempo change to the name when it helps: "Chorus 3 (up a step)".
- **The musician's locators.** They stay where they are. Where one already marks a section start, rename it in place to that section's name, so the start keeps a single locator; leave the others untouched.
- **The lead sheet's sections.** Update the existing sections in place to match the Arrangement — kind, name, start beat, length — each keeping its chords. Sections the Arrangement has and the lead sheet lacks are added, and their lyrics stay unwritten; each section appears once.
- **The lyrics.** Every sung line stays as it is. When a section is renamed, rename its label in the lyrics to match ("[Verse]" → "[Verse 1]"), so the lyrics and the Arrangement name the same sections.
- Marking sections leaves every clip, note and device as it is. A plan that changes lengths or order is carried out with the arrangement skill, and the locators and lead sheet follow.
- Keep a single plan in the Set and the lead sheet; Again gives another take, and the history keeps the old one.

### 4. Verify

Run every check that applies to the change.

| Check | What to review |
|---|---|
| Lengths | start beats and lengths describe the intended boundaries, including irregular lengths, pickups or deliberate gaps; do not round them to whole bars |
| Boundaries | each locator marks the intended section start; check downbeat alignment only where that is the chosen boundary |
| Names | labels are consistent and navigable, retaining the musician's descriptive section names; avoid duplicate locators at the same start |
| Agreement | the locators, the lead sheet's sections and the Arrangement's parts agree on where each section starts, and every label in the lyrics is the name of a section |
| Timing | start times follow from the tempo (seconds = bars × beats per bar × 60 ÷ BPM), and the first chorus and the total length suit the genre or the musician's target |
| Contrast | assess the requested degree of repetition or change in this song; no minimum number of differences is required |
| Energy | follows the intended arc, which may rise, fall, remain level or peak early; a final peak is optional |
| Kept | the musician's locators are all still there, renamed at most; locked sections are untouched; every sung line in the lyrics is as it was |

Fix unintended boundary or agreement errors and check again. Describe artistic effects without treating conventional form or energy as a pass/fail gate.

### 5. Let them hear it

When your turn ends, the musician presses play in Live. Marking sections leaves the sound as it was; after a change of lengths or order, the result line names the first section it touched, so the musician can play from just before it.

### 6. Report in one line

What changed, then " · ", then what was checked:

- "Sections marked: Intro, Verse 1, Pre-Chorus 1, Chorus 1 … Outro, 10 locators · lengths add up to 64 bars, first chorus at 0:40."
- "Verse 2 halved to 8 bars, so chorus 2 lands at 1:28 · phrases whole, energy still rising into the last chorus."
- "Form planned: 80 bars, about 3:05, first chorus at 0:37 · 12 locators on downbeats, lead sheet updated."

Describe the chosen shape and its effect when useful; a requested new form needs no warning about earlier habits.

## When to ask

Act without asking. The one question, a single line with buttons, comes only when the answer changes what Ode does:

- removing a section, or shortening one whose lines are in the lyrics, because that deletes part of the song: "Cut verse 2's last four lines to reach the chorus sooner? · Cut them · Keep them";
- rewriting the whole form of a song that already has one, or moving or deleting the musician's own locators;
- a muted track decides where a section starts, and the hand edits and version history leave it unexplained (muted long ago and untouched means parked).

The mode decides the rest: in Manual, Ode shows each change for Apply or Skip.

## Section roles

| Section | Job | Typical length | Usual traits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intro | sets key, tempo and groove; often previews the hook | 2–8 bars (16–32 for DJ tracks) | sparse; an instrumental hook or the groove |
| Verse | tells the story, with new lyrics each time | 8–16 bars | lower energy and range; same melody each time |
| Pre-chorus | builds tension and hands over to the chorus | 2–8 bars | rising melody, harmonic push, ends open |
| Chorus | the main message and the hook, repeated | 8 bars, often 8 + 8 | highest energy so far; the title; same lyric each time |
| Post-chorus | extends the chorus with a second hook | 4–8 bars | chant, vocal or instrumental hook |
| Refrain | a repeated line that closes each verse in songs without a chorus | 1–4 bars | carries the title |
| Bridge (middle 8) | contrast: a new angle in the lyric, new chords or melody | 4–8 bars, up to 16 | usually once, after the second chorus |
| Breakdown | an energy drop with elements removed | 4–16 bars | dance music; also pop before the last chorus |
| Build | rising tension into a drop or a chorus | 4–16 bars | risers, snare builds, filter sweeps |
| Drop | the peak of a dance track, often the instrumental hook | 8–32 bars | full kick and bass |
| Instrumental or solo | variation, and a rest for the singer | 4–16 bars | — |
| Outro | ends the song | 2–16 bars (16–32 for DJ tracks) | fade, hard ending, return to the intro, tag |

These are habits, and songs bend them. Most sections come in multiples of 2 or 4 bars because listeners feel phrases in groups of four.

## Bars and time

Seconds = bars × beats per bar × 60 ÷ BPM. At 120 BPM in 4/4 a bar lasts 2 seconds, so 8 bars take 16 seconds; at 96 BPM a bar lasts 2.5 seconds. Live counts positions in beats from the song's start, so bar n begins at beat (n − 1) × beats per bar. Choose section boundaries from the material and brief. Whole phrases, a shortened bar or an abrupt cut can each be appropriate; preserve intentional timing.

## Common forms

| Form | Shape | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|
| Verse–chorus | V C V C B C | pop, rock, country, R&B, singer-songwriter |
| Verse–pre-chorus–chorus | V P C V P C B C (C) | pop, rock |
| With post-chorus | V P C PC V P C PC B C PC | pop, dance-pop |
| AABA (32-bar) | A A B A, 8 bars each, often repeated for solos | jazz standards, older pop, musical theatre |
| Strophic (AAA), often with a refrain | V V V V, each ending on the title line | folk, hymns, ballads, some blues and country |
| 12-bar blues | repeated 12-bar choruses for verses and solos | blues, rock'n'roll, jazz blues |
| Rap verse and hook | intro, verse (16), hook (8), verse, hook, verse, hook, outro | hip-hop; bridges are rare |
| Dance track | intro, groove, breakdown, build, drop, breakdown, build, drop, outro | house, techno and other club music, in 8-, 16- and 32-bar phrases |
| Through-composed | new music for each section | film, art song, some progressive music |

Check the song's genre and the artist's own songs, and let the song itself suggest its form. More detail per genre is in `references/forms-by-genre.md`.

## The energy curve

Energy is how full, loud, high, dense and busy the music feels. Plan it per section on a simple 1–10 scale.

- One common rising arc puts verse 1 low, successive choruses higher and a dip before the final peak. Try it when that journey is wanted; a descending, static or early-peaking arc is equally available.
- Energy goes up with more layers, a higher register, faster subdivisions, faster harmonic rhythm, more words or a higher vocal line, louder drums and wider stereo; it comes down when the kick or bass drops out, the chords thin, the feel goes half-time, the register drops or the words thin out.
- Section boundaries are clear places for an energy change; mid-section shifts, sudden cuts and long plateaus can also be intentional.

## Contrast between sections

When more contrast is wanted, possible levers include melody register, melodic rhythm, chords or harmonic rhythm, arrangement density, drum pattern, lyric density. A lyric-only change can also define the form when that is the intended repetition. A repeated melody or chord pattern can make sections recognisable; an unchanged return or a larger transformation can also be the chosen form.

## Intros and outros

- **Intros**: start with the hook (an instrumental chorus or riff), with a stripped version of the verse groove, cold with the voice, or with a short pickup into verse 1. Pop intros are short so the voice arrives quickly; DJ tracks need longer intros with only drums and a few elements so they can be mixed.
- **Outros**: repeat the chorus or hook and fade; end hard on a downbeat with everyone together; return to the intro idea (a bookend); add a new tag line; or strip down to one element.
- Match the ending to the song's last thought: a hard stop for a punchline, a fade for something unresolved.

## When to break form

Possible departures include the following; frequent irregularity can itself be the song's language:

- add or remove a bar or two so a line can breathe or the hook arrives early (a 2-bar extension before the last chorus);
- skip the second pre-chorus or halve verse 2 to reach the chorus sooner;
- open with the chorus;
- a bar of silence or a drum stop before the final chorus;
- a false ending: the song stops, then one more chorus;
- end on the bridge or on a new section, where a chorus would usually close the song.

The result line gives the reason in musical terms: "at 120 BPM, dropping the second 8-bar pre-chorus reaches chorus 2 sixteen seconds sooner".

## Worked example: "plan the form"

A synth-pop song at 104 BPM in 4/4 (one bar ≈ 2.31 s). The Arrangement holds an 8-bar chorus loop and an 8-bar verse idea; the lyrics have a verse and a chorus; the artist's songs usually run about three minutes. The musician clicks "Plan the form".

Ode writes the plan into the lead sheet's sections and puts a locator on each start:

| Section | Start bar | Bars | Start time | Energy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Intro | 1 | 4 | 0:00 | 3 |
| Verse 1 | 5 | 8 | 0:09 | 4 |
| Pre-Chorus 1 | 13 | 4 | 0:28 | 5 |
| Chorus 1 | 17 | 8 | 0:37 | 7 |
| Post-Chorus 1 | 25 | 4 | 0:55 | 6 |
| Verse 2 | 29 | 8 | 1:05 | 5 |
| Pre-Chorus 2 | 37 | 4 | 1:23 | 6 |
| Chorus 2 | 41 | 8 | 1:32 | 8 |
| Post-Chorus 2 | 49 | 4 | 1:51 | 7 |
| Bridge | 53 | 8 | 2:00 | 4 |
| Chorus 3 | 61 | 16 | 2:18 | 9 |
| Outro | 77 | 4 | 2:55 | 3 |
| End | 81 | — | 3:05 | — |

Checks: eighty bars back to back in whole phrases, about 3:05; first chorus at 0:37, early enough for pop; every locator on a downbeat; the bridge dips to 4 so the double final chorus lands as the peak; the verse and chorus loops already differ in register and drum pattern. The arrangement skill then places the parts.

Result line: "Form planned: 80 bars, about 3:05, first chorus at 0:37 · 12 locators on downbeats, energy dips at the bridge before the last chorus."

Other takes for Again: open with the post-chorus hook as the intro (chorus at 0:28); or drop the pre-chorus and use a half-time bridge, ending on the post-chorus hook — useful alternatives when a different shape is wanted.
