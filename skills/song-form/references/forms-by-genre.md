## 1. Pop

Verse–pre-chorus–chorus with a bridge, often with a post-chorus. Reaches the chorus early and repeats it often. The pop sub-skill has the detail.

## 2. Rock and indie

- Verse–chorus with an instrumental intro built on the main riff; a guitar or instrumental solo often replaces or follows the bridge.
- Verses commonly 8–16 bars, choruses 8–16; intros 4–8 bars, often the riff that returns between sections.
- Endings: a big final chorus into a hard ending, or a repeated outro riff that fades.
- Indie often bends form: long instrumental sections, a chorus that only arrives late, or a final section that is new material.

## 3. R&B and soul

- Verse–pre-chorus–chorus, with a bridge that often modulates or strips back to voice and keys.
- Vamps: the end of the song repeats a short chord loop while the vocal ad-libs over it; plan its length in 4- or 8-bar blocks.
- Intros can be short spoken or ad-libbed openings over the groove.

## 4. Hip-hop

- Intro – Verse 1 (16 bars) – Hook (8) – Verse 2 (16) – Hook (8) – Verse 3 (16, optional) – Hook (8) – Outro.
- Verses are usually 16 bars; shorter (8 or 12) for a fast-moving or radio version; a hook is usually 8 bars and may be sung.
- Some songs open with the hook. A bridge is rare; a beat switch (a new beat for the last verse or the outro) does the job of contrast.
- Pre-hooks of 4 bars can work like a pre-chorus.

## 5. Country

- Verse–chorus with a bridge, often with a short instrumental turnaround between sections.
- Story-led: verses move the story forward in time, and the chorus line can change meaning as the story develops.
- The final chorus may repeat its last line as a tag.

## 6. Folk and singer-songwriter

- Strophic (AAA) forms with a refrain line closing each verse are common; verse–chorus is common too.
- A long strophic song stays interesting through arrangement changes per verse, an instrumental verse in the middle, a contrasting middle section, a key change for the last verse, or a final verse that is half the length or slower.
- Intros are often the accompaniment pattern for 2–4 bars; endings repeat the refrain or the last line.

## 7. Jazz standards

- 32-bar AABA (8 bars each) or ABAC (two 16-bar halves). The A sections share their melody; B (the bridge) contrasts.
- Played as: the head (the tune), solo choruses over the same form, then the head again, often with an intro, a tag ending (the last phrase repeated) or a short coda.
- 12-bar blues forms are common in jazz too (the jazz harmony sub-skill has the chords).

## 8. Blues

- Repeated 12-bar choruses: often a lyric pattern of a line, the same line repeated, and an answering line (AAB).
- Vocal choruses alternate with instrumental solo choruses; a turnaround in the last two bars leads back to the top.
- 8-bar and 16-bar blues forms also exist.

## 9. House, techno and other club music

- Built in 8-, 16- and 32-bar phrases. Elements enter and leave on phrase boundaries (bars 17, 33, 49…).
- Typical shape: Intro (16–32 bars, drums and few elements, for mixing) – Groove (16–32) – Breakdown (8–32, without the kick) – Build (4–16) – Drop (16–32) – second Breakdown and Build – second Drop – Outro (16–32, stripping back for mixing).
- At 124 BPM, 32 bars last about 62 seconds; extended club versions commonly run 5–8 minutes, shorter edits for streaming.
- Vocals, when present, often appear only in the groove and the breakdown.

## 10. Ballads in any genre

- Slower tempos make every bar longer, so sections are often shorter in bars: an 8-bar verse at 70 BPM lasts about 27 seconds.
- The emotional peak often comes late — the bridge or a final chorus with a key change or the highest note.
- Space matters: a short instrumental breath between sections lets lines land.

## 11. Songs in 3/4 and 6/8

- Count phrases in bars just the same; 4-bar and 8-bar phrases still feel natural.
- In 3/4 a bar has three beats: seconds per bar = 3 × 60 ÷ BPM. At 88 BPM a bar lasts about 2.05 seconds.
- In 6/8 the beat is usually felt as two dotted quarters per bar; state which tempo unit the BPM refers to before computing times.

## 12. Versions and edits

- Radio or streaming edit: a shorter intro, a halved second verse, fewer repeats at the end.
- Extended mix: longer DJ-friendly intro and outro, longer breakdowns.
- Instrumental and a cappella versions keep the same form, which makes them easy to line up later.
- Plan the main version first; edits come from removing whole phrases, so keep sections in clean 4- or 8-bar blocks.
