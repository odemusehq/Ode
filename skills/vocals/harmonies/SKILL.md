# Vocal harmonies

Only what is specific to harmony lines. Ranges, doubles, answers and recording preparation are in the vocals skill; notation is the same (scientific pitch, lengths in beats).

## Choosing the interval

- **A third above** the lead is the most common harmony: sweet and close.
- **A third below**, or **a sixth below** (the third above, dropped an octave): warm, and useful when the lead is already high.
- **Fourths and fifths**: open, hollow, sometimes folk-like or ancient-sounding; use them for a few notes or on purpose.
- **Octaves**: strength rather than harmony (covered under doubles in the vocals skill).

Stay in the key: a diatonic third above is sometimes a major third, sometimes a minor third, depending on the scale step.

## Fit the chord, not just the scale

A harmony that runs exactly parallel to the lead in diatonic thirds will sometimes land on a note that clashes with the chord. On strong beats and long notes, harmony notes should be chord tones (or colour tones the musician wants). When the parallel note fails:

- move it to the nearest chord tone, even if that briefly makes a fourth or a sixth with the lead;
- hold the previous note while the lead moves (oblique motion);
- or switch to the third below for that phrase.

## Motion

- **Parallel** motion (same direction, same interval) is the default and blends easily.
- **Oblique** motion (one line holds, the other moves) is the easiest way around a clash and makes the harmony simple to sing.
- **Contrary** motion (opposite directions) adds independence; use it at phrase ends and in counter-lines.
- Long runs of parallel fifths or octaves with the lead sound hollow; keep them for deliberate effects.
- Avoid crossing the lead (the upper harmony dipping below it) unless that is the idea; it confuses which line is the melody.

## Words and rhythm

- Harmonies usually sing exactly the lead's words and rhythm, so every syllable and consonant lines up.
- Harmonizing only the key words — the title, the last word of a line — often works better than harmonizing everything.
- Match vowels and phrasing to the lead so the parts blend into one sound.

## Singability of the harmony line

A harmony line has to work on its own, because someone has to sing it in tune.

- Prefer steps and held notes; smooth out awkward leaps the parallel line inherits (a held note is often better than a sixth up and back).
- Avoid tritone and major-seventh leaps.
- Keep the line inside its singer's range; a third above the lead's peak is often the first note out of range, so check the harmony stays inside the range before presenting it.

## Three-part stacks

- Lead plus a third above plus a fifth above (or the root above) makes a close-position triad: full and bright, best on held chords and key words.
- Lead plus a third below plus a sixth below puts the lead on top, which keeps it clear.
- Voice-lead the stack like chords: each part moves by the smallest step to the next chord tone.

## Where to harmonize

- Choruses and hooks first; the title is the natural place for the first harmony in a song.
- The last line of a verse, to lead into the chorus.
- Save the fullest stacks for the final chorus; a harmony everywhere stops being special.

## Worked example

A major, `| A | F♯m | D | E |`, lead:

```
Lead:              | C♯4(1) D4(1) E4(1) C♯4(1) | A3(1) B3(1) C♯4(2) | D4(1) E4(1) F♯4(1) E4(1) | E4(4) |
Parallel 3rd above:| E4(1) F♯4(1) G♯4(1) E4(1) | C♯4(1) D4(1) E4(2) | F♯4(1) G♯4(1) A4(1) G♯4(1) | G♯4(4) |
Adjusted harmony:  | E4(1) F♯4(1) A4(1) E4(1)  | C♯4(1) D4(1) F♯4(2) | F♯4(1) A4(3) | G♯4(4) |
```

What changed and why:

- Bar 1, beat 3: G♯4 is the major 7th of A on a strong beat; A4 (the root) is safer. Keep G♯4 if the musician likes that soft Amaj7 colour.
- Bar 2, beats 3–4: E4 is the 7th of F♯m on a long note; F♯4 (the root) settles it, a fourth above the lead for two beats.
- Bar 3: the parallel line's G♯4 is the ♯11 of D on beats 2 and 4, which sticks out. Holding A4 (oblique motion) keeps every note a chord tone and is easier to sing; the F♯4 at the start of the bar carries over from bar 2.
- Bar 4: G♯4, the 3rd of E, works as it is.

The adjusted line moves mostly by step, stays within C♯4–A4, and keeps the lead on the bottom of a clear pair.

## Check (harmony-specific)

- Strong-beat and long harmony notes are chord tones, or colours the musician chose.
- The harmony line is singable on its own and inside its singer's range.
- It sings the lead's words and rhythm, or only the chosen key words.
- No accidental crossing of the lead, and no long stretches of parallel fifths or octaves.
