# Vocals

The lead vocal carries the song. Everything else in the vocal arrangement — harmonies, doubles, answers, ad-libs, pads — exists to make the lead land harder, never to bury it. Plan parts the singer can actually sing, in their range, and keep each change small enough for the musician to judge.

## Conventions

- Note names use scientific pitch: middle C is C4 (MIDI 60), shown as C3 in Live. When you name a note to the musician, add its Live label the first time (for example "E5, shown as E4 in Live") so it matches their piano roll.
- Lengths are in beats of 4/4: 1 = quarter, ½ = eighth, 2 = half note; `r` is a rest and `|` a barline.
- Chords are chord symbols. "BVs" means backing vocals.

## Gather first

- The lead melody and lyric (the clips and the lyric sheet), the key, the tempo and the chords.
- Who will sing each part and each singer's comfortable range, from what they have said or sung. If it is unknown and it matters, ask. Never infer a range, or a part, from anything about the person.
- The arrangement around the voice: which instruments sit in the vocal's register, and where the gaps in the lead are.
- What is locked, and what the artist has kept or rejected in vocal arrangements before.

## Range planning

- Find the song's lowest and highest lead notes in each section and compare them with the singer's comfortable range.
- Aim for the chorus peak to sit a little below the top of their range, and the verse to sit comfortably in the lower middle.
- Check every backing part against its singer too: a harmony a third above the lead's peak is often the first note out of range.
- If the song sits badly, a key change is the cleanest fix; it changes every part, so suggest it and ask, never just do it.

Example: the artist's comfortable range is A2–E4. The chorus peak is D4, two semitones below the top, so the key works. A harmony a third above that peak would be F♯4, above the range; put that harmony a sixth below the lead instead (F♯3), or give it to another singer whose range fits.

## The parts

| Part | What it is | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| Harmony | a second line a third, sixth or other interval from the lead, on the same words | lift and colour, mostly in choruses and hooks |
| Stack | two or more harmonies forming chords with the lead | the biggest moments |
| Double | the lead sung again in unison, as a separate performance | thickness and strength on key lines |
| Octave double | the lead an octave below or above | weight (below) or brightness (above) |
| Call and response | a short answer sung in the gaps of the lead | conversation, energy in later verses and choruses |
| Echo | the end of a lead line repeated | emphasis on a title or key word |
| Ad-libs | improvised-sounding fills and embellishments | gaps, final choruses, outros |
| Gang vocals | many voices in unison or octaves, often shouted or chanted | hooks, post-choruses, chants |
| Pad | sustained vowels ("ooh", "ah") holding chord tones | warmth under verses or bridges |

The vocals/harmonies sub-skill covers writing harmony lines and stacks in detail.

## Keep the lead clear

- Backing parts use fewer words than the lead, or only its key words.
- Keep them out of the way of the lead's important words, and in a different register where possible.
- End words together. Sing final consonants ("s", "t", "k") only on the lead, or cut them sharply on backing parts, so the endings don't smear.
- Matching vowels and phrasing between the lead and its backing parts is what makes them blend.

## Doubles

- Double the lines that need strength: the chorus, the hook, the last line of a verse. Doubling everything makes the vocal sound smaller and blurred.
- A double is a separate performance. A copy of the lead shifted in time doesn't sound like a double; plan it as a part to sing.
- An octave-below double adds weight to a hook; an octave-above double brightens it if it sits comfortably in range. A whispered double adds intimacy and texture.

## Call and response

Write answers into the rests of the lead melody. Keep them short, start them after the lead's phrase ends, and contrast the lead in register or rhythm. Answers often repeat or comment on the title.

Example, D major, `| D | Bm |`:

```
Lead:     | D  | r(½) F♯4(½) A4(1) F♯4(1) E4(1) |
          | Bm | D4(2) r(2) |
Response: | Bm | r(2) B4(½) A4(½) F♯4(1) |
```

The response fills the two-beat gap in bar 2, sits above the lead's last note, and lands on chord tones of Bm on beats 3 and 4.

## Ad-libs

- Place them in the gaps, at phrase ends, over the final chorus and the outro — not on top of the lead's key words.
- Build them from words in the lyric, from vowels, and from short melodic fills on the key's pentatonic notes (in D major: D, E, F♯, A, B), which fit most chords.
- Write a few as a starting point even though the singer will improvise their own; they show where ad-libs belong and how busy they should be.

## Building across the song

- Verse 1: lead alone, or a single soft double on the last line.
- Chorus 1: doubles on the hook, one harmony on the title.
- Verse 2: answers or echoes in the gaps.
- Bridge: a different texture — a pad, a single harmony, or the lead alone for contrast.
- Final chorus: the fullest: doubles, a stack, answers and ad-libs.

Each section adds or changes one thing, so the vocal arrangement grows with the song's energy.

## Preparing for recording

- Confirm the key with the singer: have them sing the highest chorus line and the lowest verse line before the session.
- Fix the tempo, any tempo changes and the count-in.
- Prepare a lyric sheet with sections, line breaks, breath marks, and words that need care in pronunciation.
- Put the guide melody on its own MIDI track, and each backing part in its own clearly named clip (for example "BV Harmony High – Chorus"), so the singer can hear exactly what to sing.
- Plan the takes: the order of sections, how many full takes to record for comping, which lines get doubles, and where to punch in (at phrase starts).
- A demo vocal in the artist's own voice can help everyone hear the song before the session; see the demo-vocal sub-skill.

## How to work

1. Read the lead, the chords and the singers' ranges.
2. Propose two or three vocal arrangements for the section — for example doubles only; a third-above harmony on the hook; answers in the gaps — each with one sentence on what it adds. When you offer three, label one clearly as outside the artist's usual style.
3. Write the parts as separate MIDI clips the singer can follow; check them (below).
4. The musician hears them before anything lands in the Set; never change the lead or other locked parts.

## Check before presenting

- Every part sits inside its singer's comfortable range.
- Harmony notes agree with the chords on strong beats and don't clash with the lead.
- Backing parts sing the same words at the same time as the lead, unless they are deliberate answers or echoes.
- Each part is singable on its own: mostly steps, breaths in sensible places.
- The lead stays clear: backing parts avoid its key words and register where possible.
- Locked parts are untouched.
