# Singability: vowels and consonants

Detail behind the singability section of the prosody skill. The singability check reports problems on the actual melody (closed vowels on long or high notes, consonant clusters on very short notes, phrases without a rest to breathe); use this to understand them and to choose the word or note that fixes them. When a problem remains, the result line names it in plain words.

## 1. Vowels on long and high notes

| Vowel sound | As in | On long notes | On high notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| "ah" | far, calm, heart (UK) | excellent | excellent |
| "aw" | saw, call, more (UK) | excellent | very good |
| "oh" | go, home, slow | excellent | very good |
| "ay" | day, stay, rain | very good (hold the first part) | good |
| "eye" | night, why, fire | very good (hold the "ah" part) | good |
| "oo" | you, through, blue | good | harder very high |
| "ee" | free, see, dream | good in the middle of the range | harder very high; singers often open it toward "ih" |
| "eh" | bed, said, then | fair; naturally short | fair |
| "ih" | sit, live, with | poor; naturally short | poor |
| "uh" | cup, love, from | fair | fair |
| short "oo" | book, could, put | poor; naturally short | poor |
| "er" | bird, word, hurt | fair (US singers colour it with "r") | fair |

**Fixing a poor held vowel:** swap the word for one with the same meaning and a better vowel ("live" → "stay", "with" → "beside", "hit" → "hold"), move the held note to a different word in the line, or shorten the note and let a later word take the length.

## 2. Diphthongs

Some vowels glide from one sound to another: "ay" (day), "eye" (night), "oy" (boy), "ow" (down), and "oh" (go). On a held note, the singer sustains the first sound and moves to the second only at the very end:

- "night" is held as "naaah-eet";
- "down" is held as "daaah-oon";
- "day" is held as "deeeh-ee".

So the first sound decides how the note feels. "Down" holds on an open "ah" and sings well; "boy" holds on "aw" and sings well too.

## 3. Consonants

- **Plosives** (p, b, t, d, k, g) cut the sound off. They give attack and punch to short notes and rhythmic lines, but end a long note abruptly.
- **Consonants you can sing through** (m, n, ng, l, v, z, and r in US English) keep the sound going. They connect legato lines and let a held note ring or hum.
- **Sibilants** (s, sh, z, ch) hiss, especially through a microphone. Avoid them at the end of a long held note, and beware of clusters of them ("this sister's shoes").
- **Clusters** (str, nks, sixths, texts, asked) take time to articulate; on fast notes they blur. Fast passages want light, simple syllables.
- **Repeated consonants across word boundaries** merge when sung ("cold days" becomes "col-days", "black cat" becomes "bla-cat"). That's often fine, but it can hide a word the listener needs.

## 4. "R" after a vowel, UK and US

- **US English:** the "r" in "heart", "fire", "more" is pronounced. On a held note, singers usually sustain the vowel and add the "r" at the end. A word like "bird" or "hurt", whose vowel is coloured by "r" throughout, sounds tight on long high notes.
- **UK English (standard Southern British):** the "r" after a vowel is silent, so "heart" holds as a pure "ah" and "more" as a pure "aw". This makes many held notes more open in a UK accent.
- Follow the musician's setting and how they actually sing; this affects which word suits a held note as well as which words rhyme.

## 5. Melisma

A melisma stretches one syllable over several notes. Put melismas on open vowels and on words that matter emotionally; a melisma on a function word ("the", "to") or a short vowel ("it") draws attention to the wrong place and is harder to sing. When a line has fewer syllables than the melody has notes, choose where the extra notes go as carefully as the words.

## 6. Word boundaries

Sung words run together, and listeners sometimes hear a different phrase from the one written (a mondegreen). Classic examples: "a nice man" heard as "an ice man", "some others" heard as "some mothers". Read the line as continuous sound at tempo; if another phrase appears, adjust a word or the rhythm so the boundary is clear. Pay special attention to the title line, which must be heard correctly.

## 7. Breath

- Every phrase needs to fit in one breath at the song's tempo, with time to breathe before the next.
- Natural breath points are where a comma or full stop would go in speech. Avoid phrases that force a breath in the middle of a word or a tight phrase.
- High, loud and long phrases use more air. After one, give the singer a shorter phrase or a rest.
- Dense rapped or fast-sung passages need planned breath points, usually every two to four bars.
