# Syllables and stress

A melody is a row of notes with a rhythm; a lyric line is a row of syllables with a stress pattern. They fit when the counts agree (or differ in a way the melody can absorb) and when the stressed syllables land where the music puts its weight. Count alone is not enough: "BEAU-ti-ful" and "to-MOR-row" both have three syllables, but they don't fit the same three notes.

## Ground rules

- **Check every line's syllable count and stress before presenting it — never count by eye.** Words like "every", "fire", "family" and "really" trip up models and people alike; the check doesn't.
- **Report counts as the check gives them, including every possible count:** "10|11 (every 2|3)". A count like 10|11 means the line can be sung either way. Don't quietly pick one.
- **Mark uncertain words.** Names, slang and invented words may have no confirmed pronunciation; say so, and never present that line as checked.
- **Use the musician's pronunciation setting (UK or US).** If they sing in an accent that differs from the setting, ask which one to check against, and believe them about how they say their own words.
- **Keep the same pronunciation all the way through.** When a line is set to notes, use the count the check matched: if "every" was counted as two syllables, it is sung "ev-ry".
- If the check can't run, say the counts are unchecked rather than guessing.
- The example lines here only illustrate the craft; never reuse them in the musician's song.

## Why counts matter

- **Each syllable needs a note.** One syllable can stretch across several notes (a melisma), but two syllables can't comfortably share one. So a line with fewer syllables than notes can work by holding some syllables over two notes; a line with more syllables than notes needs extra notes.
- **Melodies repeat.** Verse 2 sings to verse 1's melody, so matching lines need the same count and a similar stress pattern, or the melody has to change.
- **Stress drives rhythm.** Stressed syllables want strong beats and longer notes; unstressed ones want weak beats and shorter notes (see the prosody skill). A line with the right count but the wrong stress pattern still sounds wrong.
- **Density sets the feel.** More syllables per bar sounds urgent or conversational; fewer sounds spacious or anthemic. Match the density to the section's job.

## Reading stress

- **Every word of two or more syllables has a fixed main stress:** to-MOR-row, BEAU-ti-ful, a-LONE, YES-ter-day. Singing a word against its stress ("TO-mor-row") is the most noticeable fit problem a line can have.
- **Long words also carry a secondary stress** (UN-der-STAND, CON-ver-SA-tion), which can take a medium beat.
- **Content words** (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, and negatives such as "not" and "can't") normally carry the stress in a line. **Function words** (a, the, of, to, and, in, my, your, is) are usually unstressed and weakened: "to" sounds like "tuh". Keep function words off long and high notes.
- **Stress can move for meaning.** "I didn't say YOU did it" and "I didn't SAY you did it" are different lines. Decide which word the line is really about and give it the strongest beat.
- **In replies,** show stress with capitals for stressed syllables (*i LEFT the LIGHT on*), or with a beat grid when there is a melody.

## Words with more than one sung count

Many words can be sung with different numbers of syllables. The check lists all of them; the melody decides which is used.

- **A weak vowel that drops:** every (2|3), different (2|3), family (2|3), history (2|3), memory (2|3), favourite/favorite (2|3), evening (2|3), heaven (1|2).
- **A vowel before "r" that can merge into one syllable or stay as two:** fire, hour, our, power, flower, desire (1|2).
- **Others:** "real" (1|2); in British English, an unstressed "-ia" or "-ual" ending, as in "India" or "actual", can be one syllable or two.

How to use this:
- When a line is one syllable over, a variable word may already solve it: "fits if 'every' is sung 'ev-ry'".
- When matching verses, make sure both lines use the same choice.
- Prefer the count the musician would naturally sing. Stretching "fire" into two full syllables in a fast line, or squeezing "family" into two in a slow, exposed one, can sound unnatural.

## Elisions and contractions

- **Contractions often save a syllable and sound conversational:** I am → I'm, do not → don't, will not → won't, you are → you're, it is → it's, let us → let's.
- **Not every contraction saves one.** "Should've", "could've" and "would've" are still two syllables (should-uv), and "didn't", "isn't" and "couldn't" are usually sung as two, like the full forms. Check the line again after contracting.
- **Informal forms:** going to → gonna saves one (3 → 2); want to → wanna and got to → gotta keep the count but smooth the sound. Dropping the g ("runnin'") doesn't change the count. Use these only if they're in the musician's register.
- **Sung elisions:** a weak vowel can drop when sung ("ev'ry", "heav'n", "fam'ly"). Keep the normal spelling on the lyric sheet unless the musician prefers the apostrophe, and note which count the melody uses.
- **Un-contracting** adds a syllable and adds weight: "I do not care" puts the stress on "not".
- **Running words together** ("I'm-a", "what'cha") belongs to some styles and some singers; follow the musician's habits.

## Matching counts between verses

1. Check the reference lines (usually verse 1, or the melody itself): count and stress pattern for each line.
2. Check the new lines against them, line by line.
3. Where they differ, change the words by default. Changing the melody (splitting a note to add a syllable, tying two notes to lose one, adding or dropping a pickup) is the musician's call, so ask.
4. Fix stress as well as count: the stressed syllables in the new line should land where the reference line's do.

Differences that are easy to absorb: an extra unstressed pickup syllable before the downbeat ("and", "but", "so"), which singers fit in with a quick note all the time. Differences that are hard to absorb: anything at the end of a line, where the long notes and the rhyme usually are.

## Changing a line's count without losing its meaning

First find the words that carry the meaning (the image, the rhyme word, the title) and protect them. Then adjust around them.

**To remove syllables:**
- cut filler (just, really, so, oh) and articles where the line still sounds natural;
- contract, where it really saves a syllable;
- swap a long word for a shorter one that means the same and suits the register;
- use the shorter count of a variable word;
- restructure ("the door of the car" → "the car door").

**To add syllables:**
- un-contract for emphasis;
- add a detail that means something (a colour, a name, a time) rather than filler;
- add a natural pickup word ("and", "but", "so", "now");
- choose a longer word that says the same thing;
- repeat a word for emphasis, if the style allows it.

After any change, check the count and stress again, and read the line aloud to make sure it still sounds like something a person would say.

### Worked example

The melody needs 8 syllables in a da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM pattern (stresses on the even syllables). The musician's line: *I can't remember what you said to me* — 10 syllables.

The words that matter: "can't remember" and "what you said". Options:

1. *I can't recall a word you said* — 8 syllables; stresses on CAN'T, -CALL, WORD, SAID, matching the pattern
   "Recall" keeps the meaning in two syllables instead of three; "a word" sharpens it.
2. *I've lost the words you said to me* — 8 syllables; stresses on LOST, WORDS, SAID, ME, matching the pattern
   Keeps the original ending.

Rejected: *What did you say? I can't recall* — also 8 syllables, but it stresses the first syllable, where the pattern has an unstressed one. Same count, wrong pattern.

## UK and US differences

The check follows the accent in the musician's settings; these are the kinds of difference to expect, so you can anticipate them when choosing words.

- **Some words change count.** Words ending in -ary, -ery and -ory keep a secondary stress on the ending in US English (CEM-e-ter-y, FEB-ru-ar-y), so the ending is a full syllable that can take a beat. In UK English the ending is weak and often shortened (CEM-e-try), so the UK form may have one syllable fewer.
- **Some words change stress:** garage (UK GA-rage, US ga-RAGE), ballet (UK BAL-let, US bal-LET), address (UK ad-DRESS; US often AD-dress as a noun), laboratory (UK la-BOR-a-to-ry, US LAB-ra-to-ry). A stress shift moves which syllable wants the strong beat.
- **"R" after a vowel** is pronounced in US English and silent in UK English. It doesn't usually change the count, but it changes the sound of words like "fire", "hour" and "heart" on held notes, and it changes rhymes (see the rhymes skill).
- **Vowel differences** (the "a" in "dance", "bath", "can't") don't change the count, but they change rhymes and how a held note sounds.

## Presenting counts

Show the line, its count (all possible counts), any variable or uncertain words, and whether it meets the target:

1. *Every window on the street is dark* — 9|10 (every 2|3); target 9: fits when "every" is sung as two syllables

For a whole section, list each line with its count, and point out the lines that differ from the matching section.

## Before you present

- Every count comes from the check, with all possible counts shown.
- Variable words are named, and the choice the melody needs is stated.
- Stress patterns match the reference lines or the melody, not just the counts.
- Uncertain pronunciations are marked and not presented as checked.
- The line still says what it meant, in words the musician would use.
- The UK or US setting was used, or the musician's own pronunciation where they've told you it differs.
